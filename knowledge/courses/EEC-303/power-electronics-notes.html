Chapter 1 · Orientation

Where the Course Has Got To

Before the midterm you learned how to turn AC into DC. Everything after it is about turning DC into whatever you actually want — a different DC, or an AC of your own design. One idea, and two pieces of arithmetic, carry the whole of it.

1.1 The shape of the second half

The midterm examined devices and rectifiers. You know how a thyristor latches, why it will not turn off just because you want it to, and how to find the average output of a bridge fed from the mains. That material is not finished with — it reappears in the end-term — but it is no longer the main event.

What came after the midterm is a different problem. The mains gives you a fixed 50 Hz, fixed-amplitude supply. A motor wants variable frequency. A laptop wants 19 V from a 230 V line. An electric vehicle wants to push power back into its battery when you brake. None of those is a rectifier problem, because in none of them is the output shape set by the input. You have to manufacture the output.

That is what a switched converter does, and it is the subject of every remaining chapter.

Exam note — how the marks actually fall

The end-term is full-syllabus, but it is not examined evenly. Expect roughly 20–30% of the paper on pre-midterm material and 70–80% on everything taught since. This document is built in that proportion: Chapters 2–3 are compressed revision, Chapters 4–11 are taught in full.

For context on effort: the ETE is worth 30 marks out of 100 for this course. The tutorials are worth 10 in their own right — and, as Chapter 12 shows, they are also where the exam questions come from. Doing them properly pays twice.

1.2 Every converter is the same three things

Open up a rectifier, a buck converter and a three-phase inverter and you find the same parts list: a source, some switches that connect and disconnect it on a schedule, some energy storage that smooths what the switching does, and a load. Only the schedule changes.

The useful consequence: you do not have twelve circuits to learn, you have one structure with twelve schedules. When an unfamiliar circuit appears in the exam, the question to ask is not "which circuit is this?" but "what is the switching schedule, and what does the output average to under it?"

1.3 The master picture: water in a pipe

One analogy carries this entire document, so it is worth setting up carefully.

Think of current as water flowing through a pipe. Voltage is the pressure difference pushing it along. Then:

ElectricalWaterThe behaviour this gives you
Inductor $L$A long, heavy pipe full of moving water Water has mass, so it has momentum. You cannot change the flow instantly — push harder and it accelerates. That is $v_L = L\,di/dt$: voltage sets the rate of change of current, not the current.
Capacitor $C$An open tank Flow into the tank raises its level; the level cannot jump. That is $i_C = C\,dv/dt$.
SwitchA gate you flick open and shut Open or shut, nothing between. A real switch dissipates energy only while moving between the two — which is why switching loss exists at all.
DiodeA one-way flap valve Opens by itself whenever the water tries to flow the right way. No control, no choice.

Now the payoff. Run the gate open and shut, over and over, until the system settles into a rhythm that repeats exactly — periodic steady state. In that rhythm, the water in the heavy pipe ends each cycle at precisely the speed it started, otherwise it would be gaining speed cycle after cycle and the system would not be settled. So the net push over one cycle must be zero.

That sentence, translated back to electricity, is the single most useful tool in the second half of this course.

Key result — the two balance laws

In periodic steady state, over exactly one switching period $T_s$:

$$\underbrace{\frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{T_s} v_L\,dt = 0}_{\text{volt-second balance}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{T_s} i_C\,dt = 0}_{\text{charge balance}}$$

In words: the average voltage across an inductor is zero, and the average current into a capacitor is zero. Not small — exactly zero. If either were not, the inductor current or the capacitor voltage would drift a little further every cycle, and the circuit would not be in steady state.

Why it happens

Integrate $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$ over one period: $\int_0^{T_s} v_L\,dt = L\big[i_L(T_s) - i_L(0)\big]$. Periodic steady state means $i_L(T_s) = i_L(0)$ — the waveform repeats. So the integral is zero. The same argument on $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$ gives charge balance.

Nothing here is an approximation. It is just what "repeating" means, written down.

Why does this matter so much? Because it converts a differential-equation problem into arithmetic on areas. You never have to solve for $i_L(t)$ to find the output voltage of a buck converter. You draw the inductor voltage — two flat levels, one positive and one negative — set the two areas equal, and read off the answer in one line. Chapter 5 does exactly that, three times.

Watch out — where the analogy breaks

Water is a decent picture of $L$ and $C$ and of a diode's one-way flap. It is a bad picture of a thyristor. A flap valve closes the moment the flow tries to reverse; a thyristor, once latched on, keeps conducting until the external circuit drags its current to zero and holds it there long enough — which is the whole reason forced commutation circuits exist. There is no plumbing part that behaves like that.

The analogy also says nothing about switching loss, which comes from voltage and current being large simultaneously during the transition. Water gates do not get hot when you move them.

1.4 The second tool: averaging

The other thing you will do constantly is take the average of a chopped-up waveform over one period. For a rectifier that means integrating a piece of a sine wave between two angles; for a DC–DC converter it means a weighted sum of two flat levels. Same operation, different integrand:

$$V_o = \frac{1}{T}\int_{\text{period}} v_o(t)\,dt$$

Almost every "find the average output voltage" question in this course is that integral with the right limits. Getting the limits right is the whole skill — and it is where most marks are lost, because the limits depend on which device is conducting over which interval. That is why this document draws the conduction intervals before it writes any integral.

Checkpoint

A buck converter runs with $V_{in} = 24$ V at duty ratio $D = 0.25$. Without any formula, what is $V_o$?

The switch connects the inductor to 24 V for a quarter of each cycle and to roughly 0 V for the rest. The inductor's average voltage must be zero, so the output sits at the time-weighted average of what the inductor sees on its input side: $0.25 \times 24 = 6$ V. You have just used volt-second balance without writing an integral — that is the intended way to think about it.

Devices, Latching and Commutation

Compressed, but complete enough to answer from. Everything the two midterm papers actually asked about devices, with the traps they were built around.

This chapter and the next cover the pre-midterm half. They run at roughly a quarter of the document's length because they carry roughly a quarter of the end-term's marks — but nothing has been cut that was examined. Where a derivation is sketched rather than laboured, the text says so.

2.1 Why a thyristor is not just a slow switch

A diode decides for itself. Forward-bias it and it conducts; that is the whole of its behaviour, and you get no say in the matter. A MOSFET is the opposite — it does exactly what the gate tells it, on and off, as often as you like.

The thyristor sits awkwardly between the two, and the awkwardness is the entire point of it. You can tell it when to start. You cannot tell it when to stop.

The reason is in its structure: four layers, P-N-P-N, which you can read as two transistors wired so that each one's collector feeds the other's base.

Key result — latching vs holding

Latching current $I_L$ — the anode current that must be reached while the gate pulse is still present for the loop above to become self-sustaining. It governs turn-on.

Holding current $I_H$ — the anode current below which an already-conducting thyristor drops out. It governs turn-off.

$I_L > I_H$, typically by a factor of two or three. Getting in takes more than staying in.

Watch out — this is the single most-asked distinction in the course

It appeared as a ten-part true/false block in 2025 (5 marks) and again as an MCQ in 2026. The trap is always the same: swapping latching and holding. Fix it with one sentence — "latching gets you in, holding keeps you in" — and the whole block becomes free marks.

The other half of the trap: a thyristor cannot be turned off by triggering, and forward bias alone will not turn it on (it needs a trigger as well). Statements asserting either are false.

2.2 Commutation: the only way out

Since the gate cannot switch a thyristor off, the circuit has to. Driving the anode current to zero and keeping it there long enough for the internal charge to recombine is called commutation. Two flavours:

  • Natural (line) commutation. The AC supply does the work for free — twice a cycle it reverses and pushes the current to zero. Every mains-fed rectifier in Chapter 3 relies on this, which is why those circuits need no extra components.
  • Forced commutation. With a DC supply nothing reverses on its own, so you must build a circuit that momentarily slams a reverse voltage across the device. That is what §2.3 is about.
Key result — the commutation inequality
$$t_c > t_q$$

$t_c$ is the circuit turn-off time — how long the external circuit holds the thyristor at zero or reverse voltage. $t_q$ (often $t_{off}$) is the device turn-off time, a datasheet number. If $t_c < t_q$ the device re-enters conduction the instant forward voltage returns, with no gate pulse — a commutation failure, usually a short circuit across the supply.

Exam note

Asked directly as an MCQ in 2026 (Q1.B), and as the first part — 2 marks — of the big RLE numerical in both papers (2025 Q8a, 2026 Q3a). It is two marks you should never drop.

Finding $t_c$ on a mains-fed converter

The logic is always the same, and it is worth learning as a procedure rather than a formula, because the formula changes with topology:

Procedure — circuit turn-off time
  1. Find the angle at which the thyristor stops conducting. Call it $\beta$. (If the question gives you $\beta$, use it. If conduction is continuous in a semiconverter, $\beta = \pi + \alpha$, because the incoming device commutates the outgoing one.)
  2. Find the angle at which the supply next makes that thyristor forward-biased again — for a single-phase converter, $2\pi$.
  3. The circuit has given it the interval between the two: $\;\omega t_c = 2\pi - \beta$.
  4. Divide by $\omega = 2\pi f$ to get seconds. Compare with $t_q$.
$$\boxed{\,t_c = \frac{2\pi - \beta}{\omega}\,}\qquad\text{and for continuous conduction }(\beta = \pi+\alpha):\quad t_c = \frac{\pi - \alpha}{\omega}$$
Worked example 2.1 — both midterms, same two marks

2025 Q8(a). 230 V, 50 Hz half-controlled rectifier, $R = 10\,\Omega$, $L = 10$ mH, $E = 120$ V, $\alpha = 30°$. Find the circuit turn-off time.

Conduction is continuous, so $\beta = \pi + \alpha = 210°$. Then

$$\omega t_c = 2\pi - \beta = 360° - 210° = 150° \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_c = \frac{150}{360}\times 20\ \text{ms} = \mathbf{8.33\ ms}$$

2026 Q3(a). Same question, 220 V, $L = 30$ mH, and this time $\beta = 210°$ is given. Identical arithmetic — $t_c = 8.33$ ms.

Two different papers, two different sets of numbers, one method — and note that $R$, $L$ and $E$ played no part in part (a) at all. They matter in parts (b) and (c). A reader who starts substituting into an impedance formula here has misread the question. Also note $\beta = 210° = \pi + \alpha$ exactly: the 2026 paper handed you the continuous-conduction boundary, which is a hint that parts (b) and (c) use the continuous-conduction formulae.

2.3 Forced commutation: the voltage-commutated circuit

On a DC supply you have to manufacture the reverse voltage. The standard trick is to keep a capacitor pre-charged, then dump it across the conducting thyristor.

The sequence: capacitor $C$ sits charged to $V_{in}$. Main thyristor $T_1$ carries the load current $I_o$. To turn $T_1$ off, fire the auxiliary thyristor $T_A$. This connects $C$ so that its voltage appears across $T_1$ with reverse polarity — $T_1$ switches off at once. The load current, which cannot stop (it flows through an inductive load), now flows through the capacitor instead, discharging it linearly.

That last step is the one that matters. A constant current into a capacitor is the one case where $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$ integrates trivially:

$$I_o = C\frac{dv_C}{dt} \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta t = \frac{C\,\Delta v_C}{I_o}$$

The thyristor stays reverse-biased for exactly as long as the capacitor voltage retains its reversed sign — that is, until $v_C$ has traversed from $V_{in}$ to zero.

Key result
$$t_c = \frac{C\,V_{in}}{I_o}$$

$C$ in farads, $V_{in}$ the initial capacitor voltage in volts, $I_o$ the constant load current in amperes, $t_c$ in seconds. Valid while the load current stays constant during the interval — which is the standard assumption for an inductive load.

Worked example 2.2 — 2026 Q5(a), 3 marks

$V_{in} = 200$ V, $C = 4\ \mu$F, $L = 16\ \mu$H, constant load current $100$ A. Find the circuit turn-off time for $T_1$.

$$t_c = \frac{C V_{in}}{I_o} = \frac{4\times10^{-6}\times 200}{100} = \frac{800\times10^{-6}}{100} = \mathbf{8\ \mu s}$$

Eight microseconds is a plausible, comfortable margin — typical inverter-grade thyristors specify $t_q$ in the 10–50 µs range for slow devices and a few microseconds for fast ones, so this circuit would be designed around a fast device. Notice that $L$ did not appear. The inductor's job is to recharge the capacitor with reversed polarity through a resonant half-cycle, readying it for the next commutation; it does not set $t_c$. If the examiner asks for the resonant reversal time instead, that is when $L$ enters, as $\pi\sqrt{LC}$.

For the record: $\pi\sqrt{LC} = \pi\sqrt{16\times10^{-6}\times4\times10^{-6}} = \pi\times 8\times10^{-6} = 25.1\ \mu$s.

Watch out

$t_c = CV/I_o$ assumes the capacitor discharges at constant current. That holds for a stiff inductive load. If the question gives a resistive load, the discharge is exponential and $t_c = RC\ln 2$ instead. Read which load you have been given before choosing.

2.4 Composite switches and their I–V characteristics

This topic appeared in 2025 (5 marks) and again in 2026 (4 marks), both times lifted from Tutorial 1 Q1 with the combinations shuffled. It is the clearest example in the course of the examiner's reuse habit, and it is entirely mechanical once you see the method.

The question is always: given these ideal devices wired like this, which combinations of port voltage $v$ and port current $i$ are possible? The answer is a region — or a set of lines — in the $v$–$i$ plane.

Two facts generate everything:

  • An ideal diode allows $i > 0$ only at $v = 0$, and $v < 0$ only at $i = 0$. Its characteristic is the positive-current axis plus the negative-voltage axis — an "L" lying on its back.
  • An ideal switch (MOSFET, when you are allowed to control it) is either a short — any $i$ at $v=0$ — or an open — any $v$ at $i=0$.
Procedure — any composite switch
  1. Ask: with the switch ON, which way can current flow? One direction gives a single vertical stroke; both give a full vertical line.
  2. Ask: with the switch OFF, which polarity of voltage can it hold off? One gives a single horizontal stroke; both give a full horizontal line.
  3. Draw those strokes. That is the characteristic — nothing else is reachable.
  4. Sanity check: an anti-parallel diode always adds reverse current capability and removes reverse voltage blocking. A series diode does the opposite.

2.5 Switching loss

An ideal switch dissipates nothing: when it is on, $v = 0$; when it is off, $i = 0$; either way the product $vi$ is zero. Real devices take time to cross between those two states, and during the crossing both $v$ and $i$ are substantial at once. The energy lost is the area under their product:

$$E_{on} = \int_0^{t_{sw}} v_{sw}(t)\, i_{sw}(t)\, dt$$

For the usual idealisation — voltage falling linearly from $V$ to $0$ while current rises linearly from $0$ to $I$, both over the same interval $t_{sw}$:

$$E_{on} = \int_0^{t_{sw}} V\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{t_{sw}}\right)\cdot I\frac{t}{t_{sw}}\,dt = VI\,t_{sw}\int_0^1 (1-x)x\,dx = \boxed{\tfrac{1}{6}\,V I\, t_{sw}}$$
Worked example 2.3 — 2026 Q7, 5 marks

A switch turns on with $V = 100$ V collapsing and $I = 200$ A rising, over a transition of $100\ \mu$s. Find the turn-on energy loss.

$$E_{on} = \tfrac{1}{6}\times 100 \times 200 \times 100\times10^{-6} = \tfrac{1}{6}\times 2 = \mathbf{0.333\ J}$$

A third of a joule per switching event sounds negligible until you multiply by frequency: at 10 kHz that is 3.3 kW of heat, which no device survives. This is the reason high-power converters switch slowly and high-frequency converters handle small powers — the whole design tension of the subject in one number.

Read the figure before using the $\frac16$ formula. It only holds when the two ramps overlap over the same interval. If the paper draws the voltage falling completely before the current starts to rise, the overlap — and the idealised loss — is zero; if the ramps overlap over only part of their length, integrate the product over the overlap region by hand. The method, not the formula, is what is being marked.

2.6 Peak reverse voltage — and why the filter changes the answer

Both 2025 MCQs on this were testing one idea: a device in the off state sees the difference between the node it is tied to and the node it came from. Change what holds those nodes and you change the answer.

"Bridge rectifier PIV = $V_m$" is the half-remembered rule that loses the mark. It is only true without a capacitive filter. Add the capacitor and it doubles. The 2025 paper asked both versions, one mark each, back to back — it was testing exactly this discrimination.

Checkpoint

In a full-bridge rectifier with a capacitive filter, you increase $C$. What happens to the peak diode current?

It increases (2025 Q2, answer C). A bigger capacitor holds the output closer to $V_{s,pk}$, so the diodes conduct over a shorter slice near the peak — but they must still deliver the same average load charge. Same charge, less time, higher peak. This is the standard trade in filter design: smoother output voltage is paid for in diode current stress.

Chapter 3 · Revision pre-midterm

Rectifiers and the Control Laws

Four questions carried 30+ marks across the two midterm papers, and all four were rectifiers. They will be back in the end-term, scaled down. Here is all of it, compressed.

3.1 The one idea: firing angle selects a slice of the sine

A diode bridge has no choice — it conducts the moment the supply forward-biases it, so the output is whatever the mains hands you. Replace the diodes with thyristors and you gain exactly one degree of freedom: you choose when in the cycle to start conducting. Delay the start and you capture a smaller slice of the sine, so the average output falls. That delay, measured in degrees from the instant the device could have turned on, is the firing angle $\alpha$.

Every control law in this chapter is that same integral with different limits:

$$V_o = \frac{1}{\text{period of the output}}\int_{\text{conduction}} v_s(\theta)\,d\theta$$
Key result — the three control laws worth memorising
Converter$V_o$Range of $\alpha$
1-φ semiconverter (half-controlled)$\dfrac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$$0$ to $\pi$
1-φ full converter$\dfrac{2V_{s,pk}}{\pi}\cos\alpha$$0$ to $\pi$
3-φ full converter$\dfrac{3V_{LL,pk}}{\pi}\cos\alpha$$0$ to $\pi$

$V_{s,pk}$ is the peak of the single-phase supply, $V_{LL,pk}$ the peak line-to-line voltage of the three-phase supply. For a mains figure quoted as "230 V, 50 Hz", that is an RMS value: $V_{s,pk} = 230\sqrt2 = 325.3$ V.

Watch out — the semiconverter never goes negative

$(1+\cos\alpha)$ cannot be negative, so a half-controlled bridge can only rectify. The full converter's $\cos\alpha$ goes negative beyond $\alpha = 90°$, which lets it push power back into the AC side — it inverts. That difference is the whole reason both topologies exist, and it is the kind of one-line distinction this examiner likes as an MCQ.

3.2 The trap: a non-sinusoidal input

Both papers opened their long section with the same ambush. A single-phase semiconverter, a request for "the waveform and average output voltage" — and an input that is not a sine wave. It is a trapezoid, drawn in the question.

A reader who has memorised $\frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$ and nothing else gets zero. That formula is not a law of nature; it is the result of integrating $V_{s,pk}\sin\theta$ between $\alpha$ and $\pi$. Change the input and you must redo the integral.

Why it happens

The semiconverter's output over one half-period is: follow the input from $\alpha$ to $\pi$, then sit at zero from $\pi$ to $\pi+\alpha$ while the load freewheels through the top two devices. Averaged over the half-period $\pi$:

$$V_o = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_\alpha^\pi v_s(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Everything that distinguishes the sinusoidal case from the trapezoidal case lives inside $v_s(\theta)$. The structure outside the integral never changes.

Worked example 3.1 — 2026 Q2, 6 marks

Semiconverter fed from a trapezoid of period 20 ms: rises linearly from 0 at $\theta=0$ to $+200$ V at $\theta=90°$, holds at $+200$ V until $180°$, then falls linearly to $-200$ V at $270°$ and holds. Find $V_o$ for $\alpha = 30°$ and $\alpha = 150°$.

Step 1 — write the input as a function. On $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$ the ramp passes through $(0,0)$ and $(\pi/2,\,200)$, so $v_s = \dfrac{400\theta}{\pi}$. On $\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi$, $v_s = 200$.

Step 2 — $\alpha = 30° = \pi/6$. The firing angle lands on the ramp, so the integral splits:

$$\int_{\pi/6}^{\pi/2}\frac{400\theta}{\pi}d\theta = \frac{400}{\pi}\left[\frac{\theta^2}{2}\right]_{\pi/6}^{\pi/2} = \frac{200}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{4}-\frac{\pi^2}{36}\right) = 200\pi\cdot\frac{8}{36} = \frac{400\pi}{9} = 139.6$$ $$\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}200\,d\theta = 200\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = 100\pi = 314.2$$ $$V_o = \frac{139.6 + 314.2}{\pi} = \frac{453.8}{\pi} = \mathbf{144.4\ V}$$

Step 3 — $\alpha = 150° = 5\pi/6$. Now the firing angle lands on the flat top, past the ramp entirely, so there is only one piece:

$$V_o = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{5\pi/6}^{\pi}200\,d\theta = \frac{200}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{200}{6} = \mathbf{33.3\ V}$$

Two things are being tested and only one of them is integration. The first is noticing that at $\alpha=30°$ the limit falls inside the ramp and the integral must be split, while at $\alpha=150°$ it does not — the shape of the answer changes with $\alpha$. The second is that the familiar $(1+\cos\alpha)$ result is nowhere in sight. If you had used it with $V_{s,pk}=200$ you would have got 118.8 V and 11.6 V, both wrong, with no way of noticing.

Procedure — any converter, any input waveform
  1. Sketch the input and mark $\alpha$ on it.
  2. Mark the conduction intervals — which device conducts when. For a semiconverter: supply from $\alpha$ to $\pi$, freewheel (output = 0) from $\pi$ to $\pi+\alpha$.
  3. Write $v_s(\theta)$ piecewise over the conducting interval.
  4. Integrate piece by piece, splitting wherever the input's definition changes.
  5. Divide by the output period ($\pi$ for a semiconverter, since the pattern repeats twice per supply cycle).

3.3 The RLE numerical — ten marks in both papers

A back-EMF load ($E$) is a battery or a DC motor armature: it pushes back. The circuit only delivers current when the rectified voltage exceeds $E$.

Key result
$$V_o = \frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)\qquad\qquad I_o = \frac{V_o - E}{R}$$

$L$ does not appear in the average current. It smooths the ripple and decides whether conduction is continuous, but in steady state the average voltage across an inductor is zero (Chapter 1), so it cannot change the average current.

Worked example 3.2 — 2025 Q8 and 2026 Q3, side by side

2025: 230 V, 50 Hz, $R=10\,\Omega$, $L=10$ mH, $E=120$ V, $\alpha=30°$.

$$V_{s,pk} = 230\sqrt2 = 325.3\ \text{V}$$ $$V_o = \frac{325.3}{\pi}(1+\cos 30°) = 103.5 \times 1.866 = \mathbf{193.2\ V}$$ $$I_o = \frac{193.2-120}{10} = \mathbf{7.32\ A}$$

2026: 220 V, $R=10\,\Omega$, $L=30$ mH, $E=120$ V, $\alpha=30°$, $\beta=210°$.

$$V_{s,pk} = 220\sqrt2 = 311.1\ \text{V}$$ $$V_o = \frac{311.1}{\pi}(1.866) = 99.0\times1.866 = \mathbf{184.8\ V} \qquad I_o = \frac{184.8-120}{10} = \mathbf{6.48\ A}$$

The inductance tripled between the two papers and changed nothing in the answer — confirming the point in the box above. What did change was the supply, by 10 V RMS, which moved the output by 8 V and the current by nearly 0.9 A. Notice how sensitive $I_o$ is: the subtraction of $E$ removes most of the voltage, so a small error in $V_o$ becomes a large fractional error in $I_o$. Carry four figures until the end.

3.4 The three-phase bridge — waveforms, harmonics, power factor

Ten marks in 2025, ten again in 2026. Three sub-parts each time: draw the waveforms, say something about harmonics, compute a current ratio or the power factor.

Six diodes, two conducting at any instant — the one from the top group whose phase is most positive, and the one from the bottom group whose phase is most negative. Each device therefore holds the rail for $120°$ and then hands over.

3.4.1  Device currents

Each diode carries $I_o$ for $120°$ out of $360°$, i.e. one third of the time:

$$I_{D,avg} = \frac{I_o}{3} \qquad\qquad I_{D,rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}\,I_o = \frac{I_o}{\sqrt3} = 0.577\,I_o$$

Both follow from the definition directly — a block of height $I_o$ occupying a third of the period has average $I_o/3$, and mean-square $I_o^2/3$.

3.4.2  Why the third harmonic is absent — 2 marks, both papers

A rectangular block of height $I_o$ and width $\gamma$, repeating with half-wave symmetry, has harmonic amplitudes

$$I_n = \frac{4I_o}{n\pi}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\gamma}{2}\right)$$

For the three-phase bridge, $\gamma = 120°$, so $\dfrac{n\gamma}{2} = 60n°$ and

$$I_n = \frac{4I_o}{n\pi}\sin(60n°)$$
Key result — the triplens vanish

$\sin(60n°) = 0$ whenever $n$ is a multiple of 3: $\sin 180° = \sin 360° = \sin 540° = 0$. So the 3rd, 9th, 15th… harmonics are identically zero, and half-wave symmetry has already removed all even harmonics. What survives is $n = 1, 5, 7, 11, 13, \dots$ — i.e. $n = 6k \pm 1$.

That is the whole proof, and it is two marks. Write the formula, substitute $n=3$, state that $\sin 180° = 0$. The physical gloss, if you want one: the triplen components of the three phases are all in phase with each other, so in a three-wire system with no neutral they have nowhere to flow.

3.4.3  Power factor

From the same series, the fundamental amplitude is $I_1 = \frac{4I_o}{\pi}\sin 60° = \frac{2\sqrt3 I_o}{\pi}$, so its RMS value is

$$I_{s1,rms} = \frac{2\sqrt3\,I_o}{\sqrt2\,\pi} = \frac{\sqrt6\,I_o}{\pi} = 0.780\,I_o$$

The total RMS of the line current: the waveform is at $\pm I_o$ for $240°$ out of $360°$, so

$$I_{s,rms} = I_o\sqrt{\tfrac{240}{360}} = I_o\sqrt{\tfrac23} = 0.816\,I_o$$ $$\frac{I_{s1,rms}}{I_{s,rms}} = \frac{\sqrt6/\pi}{\sqrt{2/3}} = \frac{\sqrt6}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac32} = \frac{3}{\pi} = \mathbf{0.955}$$

This ratio is the distortion factor. The overall power factor is

$$\text{PF} = \underbrace{\cos\alpha}_{\text{displacement}}\times \underbrace{\frac{3}{\pi}}_{\text{distortion}}$$

For a diode bridge $\alpha = 0$, so $\text{PF} = 3/\pi = 0.955$.

Worked example 3.3 — 2025 Q7(c) and 2026 Q6(c)

2025 asked for the power factor; 2026 asked for the ratio $I_{s1,rms}/I_{s,rms}$ alone. Both are the lines above. For a diode bridge both answers are $3/\pi = 0.955$, because the displacement factor is 1.

0.955 is a good power factor for an uncontrolled rectifier, and all of the shortfall is distortion — the current is in phase with the voltage, it is merely the wrong shape. That is why you cannot fix a rectifier's power factor with a capacitor bank the way you can fix a motor's: there is no phase shift to cancel. You have to change the current waveform, which is exactly what the power-factor-correction stage in Chapter 6 does. Note also that if this were a controlled bridge at $\alpha=60°$, PF would fall to $0.5\times0.955 = 0.477$ — phase control buys you output voltage and pays for it in power factor.

3.5 Continuous or discontinuous?

With an inductive load the current may or may not reach zero before the next device fires. The test the examiner uses:

Key result

With $\alpha$ the firing angle and $\beta$ the extinction angle (where the current actually reaches zero):

  • Continuous if $(\beta-\alpha) \ge \pi$ — the current never gets to zero before the next device takes over.
  • Discontinuous if $(\beta-\alpha) < \pi$ — there is a dead gap.

In a 1-φ semiconverter with $\beta > \pi$ and discontinuous conduction, each thyristor conducts for $(\beta - \alpha)$.

Exam note

2026 Q1.A and Q1.D, one mark each, tested exactly these two statements. They are free marks if you have the inequality the right way round, and they are gone if you do not. There is nothing to derive — learn the two lines.

Q1.C of the same paper: each diode of a 3-φ, 3-pulse half-wave rectifier conducts for $120°$. Same reasoning as the bridge: three devices sharing one cycle, each taking the most-positive phase in turn.

3.6 Switch RMS current — and a look forward

Both papers closed with the same question type, and it is the natural bridge into the second half of the course, because the circuits in it are not rectifiers at all. 2025 drew a DC source, a switch $S_1$, a freewheeling diode and a constant current load — a buck converter. 2026 drew a full H-bridge — a single-phase inverter. You were already being examined on Chapters 5 and 7 without being told.

The method is pure definition. RMS means root of the mean of the square, and the mean is taken over the whole period, including the intervals when the device carries nothing:

$$I_{S1,rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{T_s} i_{S1}^2(t)\,dt}$$
Worked example 3.4 — 2026 Q8, 5 marks

$S_1$ and $S_4$ conduct for $DT_s$, carrying the load current; $S_2$ and $S_3$ conduct for $(1-D)T_s$. The load current is

$$I_o = \begin{cases} 3 + 4t, & 0 < t < DT_s\\[2pt] 1 - 2t, & DT_s < t < T_s\end{cases}$$

$S_1$ carries current only during the first interval, and nothing during the second. So the second piece of $I_o$ never enters $S_1$'s integral:

$$I_{S1,rms}^2 = \frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{DT_s}(3+4t)^2\,dt = \frac{1}{T_s}\left[\frac{(3+4t)^3}{12}\right]_0^{DT_s}$$ $$\boxed{\,I_{S1,rms} = \sqrt{\frac{(3+4DT_s)^3 - 27}{12\,T_s}}\,}$$

The examiner gave two pieces of $I_o$ and only one of them is used — that is the trap, and it is why the question is worth 5 marks rather than 2. The second piece is there for the complementary switch $S_2$. If you are asked for $I_{S2,rms}$, integrate $(1-2t)^2$ from $DT_s$ to $T_s$ instead, over the same $1/T_s$.

Keep the answer symbolic unless numbers for $D$ and $T_s$ are given. The 2025 version of this question used general coefficients $b+at$ and $c-dt$, which makes the symbolic form the only possible answer — more evidence that the method, not the arithmetic, is what carries the marks.

Watch out

Two mistakes cost most of the marks here. First, integrating $I_o$ over the whole period for a switch that only conducts part of it. Second, dividing by $DT_s$ instead of $T_s$ — that would give the RMS of the pulse, not of the switch current, and the two differ by a factor $\sqrt D$. The averaging window is always the full switching period.

Chapter 4 post-midterm · core

Three-Phase Controlled Rectifiers

Tutorial 5 is entirely about one circuit, and the quiz then tested that circuit's single most memorable number. This chapter derives where that number comes from, and the two different formulas either side of it.

4.1 Why bother with three phases

A single-phase rectifier has a problem you cannot design away: twice per cycle its input voltage passes through zero, so twice per cycle it has nothing to give. The output sags, and you need a large inductor or capacitor to carry the load across the gap.

Three phases, each peaking at a different moment, means something is always near its peak. There is no instant when all three are small. The output of a three-phase rectifier therefore never falls anywhere near zero — the three-pulse circuit below dips only to half its peak, and the six-pulse bridge of §3.4 dips only to 86% of its peak. Less ripple, smaller filter, more power through the same silicon.

4.2 The three-pulse (half-wave) controlled rectifier

Three thyristors, one per phase, all cathodes tied together to form the positive output rail; the load returns to the supply neutral. At any moment the thyristor that could conduct is the one whose phase is most positive — but it will not conduct until you fire it.

Why it happens — where $\alpha$ is measured from

Take $v_a = V_{s,pk}\sin\theta$. Phase $a$ is not the most positive from $\theta = 0$; it overtakes phase $c$ at $\theta = 30°$ and is overtaken by phase $b$ at $\theta = 150°$. So $30°$ is the earliest instant at which T1 could conduct — the natural commutation point — and it is from there, not from the voltage zero crossing, that the firing angle $\alpha$ is counted.

Getting this reference wrong shifts every subsequent integration limit by $30°$ and is the most common way to lose the whole question.

With the firing angle counted that way, T1 conducts from $30° + \alpha$, and — if the load current never falls to zero — hands over to T2 exactly $120°$ later. Each device takes a third of the cycle, which is the answer to the 2026 MCQ about three-pulse conduction angle.

4.2.1  Continuous conduction: the first regime

Average the output over one pulse, which lasts $2\pi/3$:

$$V_o = \frac{3}{2\pi}\int_{\pi/6+\alpha}^{5\pi/6+\alpha} V_{s,pk}\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}\Big[\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\alpha) - \cos(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}+\alpha)\Big]$$

The bracket is the one step worth flagging. Use $\cos A - \cos B = -2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\sin\frac{A-B}{2}$ with $\frac{A+B}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}+\alpha$ and $\frac{A-B}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{3}$:

$$-2\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\alpha\right)\sin\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = 2\cos\alpha\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sqrt3\cos\alpha$$
Key result — three-pulse, continuous conduction
$$V_o = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi}\,V_{s,pk}\cos\alpha = 0.827\,V_{s,pk}\cos\alpha$$

$V_{s,pk}$ is the peak of one phase voltage. Equivalently, since $V_{LL,pk} = \sqrt3\,V_{s,pk}$, this is $\dfrac{3}{2\pi}V_{LL,pk}\cos\alpha$.

Holds for $0 \le \alpha \le 30°$ with a resistive load, and for all $\alpha$ when the load current is stiff enough never to reach zero — a large $L$, or the "constant current load" the tutorials specify.

4.2.2  Discontinuous conduction: the second regime

With a resistive load the current is a scaled copy of the voltage, so the instant $v_a$ crosses zero going negative — at $\theta = 180°$ — the current reaches zero and T1 switches off on its own. It cannot wait for T2.

For $\alpha > 30°$ the firing instant $30° + \alpha$ comes late enough that the $120°$ conduction window would run past $180°$. It gets truncated there instead:

$$V_o = \frac{3}{2\pi}\int_{\pi/6+\alpha}^{\pi} V_{s,pk}\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}\Big[1 + \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\alpha\right)\Big]$$
Key result — three-pulse, R load, $30° \le \alpha \le 150°$
$$V_o = \frac{3\,V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}\Big[1 + \cos(30° + \alpha)\Big]$$

Set $\alpha = 150°$ and the bracket becomes $1 + \cos 180° = 0$. The output reaches zero at $\alpha = 150°$, and that is the firing-angle limit.

Exam note — the tutorial teaches it, the quiz tests its endpoint

Tutorial 5 Q1 asks for exactly these two derivations, split at $\alpha = 30°$. Quiz Q1 then asks, as a one-mark MCQ, for the firing-angle range of a three-phase half-wave converter feeding a resistive load — answer 0° to 150°.

This is the examiner's method in miniature, and he admitted it in writing on that quiz: "Same as in the tutorial & discussions shared, only values are changed." Work the tutorial and you get both marks.

Checkpoint — do the two formulas agree?

They must, at the boundary $\alpha = 30°$, or one of them is wrong.

Continuous: $0.827\cos 30° = 0.827 \times 0.866 = 0.716$. Discontinuous: $\frac{3}{2\pi}[1+\cos 60°] = 0.4775 \times 1.5 = 0.716$. Identical. Always run this check after deriving a piecewise result — it costs ten seconds and catches a dropped factor immediately.

4.3 Tutorial 5 Q2, worked — power into the load

Worked example 4.1 — Tutorial 5 Q2

A three-phase half-wave SCR converter delivers a constant load current of 30 A over a firing-angle range of 0° to 80°. The AC input is 400 V from a delta–star transformer. Find the power delivered to the load at the two extreme firing angles.

Step 1 — the transformer step the question does not spell out. "400 V" is the line voltage. The secondary is star connected, and a three-pulse rectifier works from phase voltages measured to the star point:

$$V_{ph,rms} = \frac{400}{\sqrt3} = 230.9\ \text{V} \qquad V_{s,pk} = 230.9\sqrt2 = 326.6\ \text{V}$$

Step 2 — which regime? The load current is constant, so it never reaches zero and conduction is continuous at every firing angle. The $\cos\alpha$ formula applies throughout — the $\alpha > 30°$ discontinuous formula does not apply here.

Step 3 — the two operating points.

$$\alpha = 0°:\quad V_o = 0.827 \times 326.6 \times 1 = 270.1\ \text{V}$$ $$\alpha = 80°:\quad V_o = 0.827 \times 326.6 \times \cos 80° = 270.1 \times 0.1736 = 46.9\ \text{V}$$

Step 4 — power. With constant current, $P = V_o I_o$:

$$P(0°) = 270.1 \times 30 = \mathbf{8.10\ kW} \qquad\qquad P(80°) = 46.9 \times 30 = \mathbf{1.41\ kW}$$

Delaying the firing by 80° has cut the delivered power to about a sixth, with the current held exactly the same — that is phase control doing its job. Two things are worth noticing. First, the trap is in step 1, not the physics: a reader who uses 400 V as the phase voltage overstates every answer by $\sqrt3$, about 73%. Second, "constant load current" is not decoration — it is the phrase that tells you conduction is continuous and licenses the $\cos\alpha$ formula at $\alpha = 80°$, well past the 30° break. With a resistive load at 80° you would have had to use the other formula and would have got a different number.

Procedure — any three-phase controlled rectifier numerical
  1. Convert the supply figure to the peak phase voltage. Line to phase: divide by $\sqrt3$ if the connection is star. RMS to peak: multiply by $\sqrt2$. Write down $V_{s,pk}$ before anything else.
  2. Decide the regime. Constant / highly inductive current → continuous, always. Resistive load → continuous only while $\alpha \le 30°$ (three-pulse) or $\alpha \le 60°$ (six-pulse bridge).
  3. Pick the matching formula from the Key Result boxes.
  4. Compute $V_o$, then $I_o = (V_o - E)/R$ if there is a back-EMF, or use the given current if it is stiff.
  5. $P = V_o I_o$ for a constant current; $P = I_{o,rms}^2 R$ if the load is purely resistive and the current ripples.

4.4 Tutorial 5 Q3 — the waveform across the thyristor

Drawing $v_o$ is routine. Drawing $v_{T1}$ is the part people skip, and it is therefore the part worth marks. The trick is a single equation:

$$v_{T1} = v_a - v_o$$

T1's anode sits at $v_a$; its cathode sits on the output rail, at $v_o$. So:

  • While T1 conducts, $v_o = v_a$, so $v_{T1} = 0$.
  • While T2 conducts, $v_o = v_b$, so $v_{T1} = v_a - v_b = v_{ab}$.
  • While T3 conducts, $v_o = v_c$, so $v_{T1} = v_a - v_c = v_{ac}$.

The voltage across a blocked thyristor is therefore built out of line voltages, not phase voltages — which is why its peak is $V_{LL,pk} = \sqrt3\,V_{s,pk}$ and not $V_{s,pk}$.

Watch out

Two reliable ways to lose these marks. First, measuring $\alpha$ from the phase voltage's zero crossing instead of from the $30°$ natural commutation point. Second, drawing $v_{T1}$ as a chopped phase voltage. It is a chopped line voltage, and the $\sqrt3$ difference in its peak is usually what the mark is for.

Chapter 5 post-midterm · core

Source Inductance and Commutation Overlap

Everything so far assumed current can jump from one device to another instantly. It cannot. The real supply has inductance, the handover takes time, and during that time the output voltage is lower than every formula in Chapter 4 predicts.

5.1 The assumption that quietly fails

Go back to the water picture from Chapter 1. Current is water in a pipe, and the pipe has mass. Now ask a rectifier to do what every analysis so far has assumed: at the instant of commutation, stop the flow dead in one pipe and start it at full rate in another.

You cannot. Stopping the water in the outgoing pipe instantly would need infinite force, because $v = L\,di/dt$ and an instantaneous change in $i$ demands an infinite $v$. Real supplies have series inductance $L_s$ — from transformer leakage, from the cables, from the generator windings — and that inductance sets how fast the handover can happen.

So for a short interval, both devices conduct at once. The outgoing current ramps down while the incoming one ramps up, and their sum stays equal to the load current. The angle over which this happens is the overlap angle $\mu$ (also called the commutation angle).

5.2 Deriving the overlap angle

Take a single-phase full converter with a stiff load current $I_o$ and source inductance $L_s$. At $\omega t = \alpha$, T3 and T4 are fired while T1 and T2 are still conducting.

Why it happens — the supply is shorted during overlap

With all four devices on, the AC source sees a short circuit through the bridge. Every volt of $v_s$ appears across the source inductance, and its entire job is to change the supply current:

$$v_s = L_s\frac{di_s}{dt}$$

Over the overlap the supply current must swing from $-I_o$ (feeding the load one way) all the way to $+I_o$ (feeding it the other) — a total change of $2I_o$, not $I_o$.

Integrate both sides over the overlap, converting $dt$ to $d(\omega t)$:

$$\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha+\mu} V_{s,pk}\sin\omega t\;d(\omega t) = \omega L_s\!\!\int_{-I_o}^{+I_o}\! di_s$$ $$V_{s,pk}\big[\cos\alpha - \cos(\alpha+\mu)\big] = 2\,\omega L_s I_o$$
Watch out — the limits are the whole question

The lecture notes stop and flag this step specifically, and it is worth repeating: the angle integral runs $\alpha \to \alpha+\mu$, and the current integral runs $-I_o \to +I_o$. That second pair of limits is where the factor of two comes from. Integrate from $0$ to $I_o$ instead — easy to do if you are thinking about the incoming device rather than the supply — and every answer after it is half what it should be.

If you compare notes with someone whose copy has $\omega L_s I_o$ where this has $2\omega L_s I_o$, that missing 2 is the reason.

Key result — the overlap angle
$$\cos(\alpha+\mu) = \cos\alpha - \frac{2\omega L_s I_o}{V_{s,pk}} \qquad\text{(single-phase full converter)}$$ $$\cos(\alpha+\mu) = \cos\alpha - \frac{2\omega L_s I_o}{V_{LL,pk}} \qquad\text{(three-phase full converter)}$$

Same structure both times; only the commutating voltage changes. In the three-phase bridge the handover is between two phases, so what drives it is the line voltage — the lecture notes reach this by applying KCL at the common-cathode node and finding $L_s\,di_{sa}/dt = \tfrac{1}{2}V_{LL,pk}\sin(\omega t - 30°)$.

Rearranged: $\mu = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\cos\alpha - \dfrac{2\omega L_s I_o}{V}\right) - \alpha$.

Read that result physically. $\mu$ grows with $L_s$ (a floppier supply hands over more slowly), grows with $I_o$ (more current to transfer), and grows with $\omega$. It also grows as $\alpha$ approaches $90°$, because the commutating voltage available to push the current across is smallest there.

5.3 The voltage you lose

Chapter 1 said these problems reduce to arithmetic on areas, and this is the cleanest example. The notch in Figure 5.1 has an area, that area is missing from every cycle, and the average output falls by exactly that area divided by the period.

For the single-phase converter the output just starts late: conduction begins at $\alpha+\mu$ instead of $\alpha$, because until the handover finishes the output is held down. So integrate the output over what is actually left:

$$V_o = \frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}\int_{\alpha+\mu}^{\pi+\alpha}\sin\omega t\;d(\omega t) = \frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}\big[\cos(\alpha+\mu) + \cos\alpha\big]$$

Now substitute the overlap relation $\cos(\alpha+\mu) = \cos\alpha - \frac{2\omega L_s I_o}{V_{s,pk}}$ and $\mu$ disappears entirely:

Key result — the average voltage drop
$$V_o = \underbrace{\frac{2V_{s,pk}}{\pi}\cos\alpha}_{\text{without source inductance}} - \underbrace{\frac{2\omega L_s}{\pi}I_o}_{\text{drop due to } L_s}$$

The elegance here is worth pausing on. The overlap angle $\mu$ was needed to get the result, and then it cancelled out of it. You do not need to know $\mu$ to predict the output voltage — only $L_s$ and the current. That is why the drop can be drawn as a simple straight line.

Why it happens — counting the notches

For the three-phase bridge: during overlap the output sits at the mean of the two commutating phases instead of following the higher one, so the height lost is $\frac{1}{2}(v_{higher} - v_{lower}) = \frac{1}{2}v_{LL}$. The area of one notch is therefore $\frac12\int v_{LL}\,d\theta = \frac12(2\omega L_s I_o) = \omega L_s I_o$.

There are six such notches per supply cycle, so the average drop is $\dfrac{6\,\omega L_s I_o}{2\pi} = \dfrac{3\omega L_s I_o}{\pi}$. The three-pulse circuit has three notches per cycle, giving half that. The counting is the derivation.

Watch out — this drop is not a resistance

$\frac{3\omega L_s I_o}{\pi}$ has the units of volts and grows linearly with $I_o$, so it behaves like a resistive drop of $\frac{3\omega L_s}{\pi}$ ohms, and textbooks often call it the "equivalent commutation resistance". It is not a resistance: it dissipates no power. No energy is lost, it is merely not delivered. Say this if asked — it is the kind of distinction that separates a full-mark answer from a three-quarter one.

Worked example 5.1 — the standard shape of this question

A three-phase full converter runs from a 415 V, 50 Hz supply with source inductance $L_s = 1.5$ mH per phase, delivering a constant 40 A at a firing angle of $30°$. Find the overlap angle and the actual average output voltage.

Step 1 — peak line voltage. $V_{LL,pk} = 415\sqrt2 = 586.9$ V, and $\omega = 2\pi(50) = 314.16$ rad/s.

Step 2 — the commutation term.

$$\frac{2\omega L_s I_o}{V_{LL,pk}} = \frac{2(314.16)(1.5\times10^{-3})(40)}{586.9} = \frac{37.70}{586.9} = 0.0642$$

Step 3 — overlap angle.

$$\cos(30° + \mu) = \cos 30° - 0.0642 = 0.8660 - 0.0642 = 0.8018$$ $$30° + \mu = 36.70° \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mu = \mathbf{6.70°}$$

Step 4 — ideal output, then the drop.

$$V_{o,ideal} = \frac{3}{\pi}(586.9)\cos 30° = 560.5 \times 0.8660 = 485.4\ \text{V}$$ $$\Delta V = \frac{3\omega L_s I_o}{\pi} = \frac{3(314.16)(1.5\times10^{-3})(40)}{\pi} = \frac{56.55}{\pi} = 18.0\ \text{V}$$ $$V_o = 485.4 - 18.0 = \mathbf{467.4\ V}$$

The overlap is under seven degrees and costs about 3.7% of the output — small, but not negligible, and it gets worse exactly when you least want it: double the load current and both $\mu$ and $\Delta V$ roughly double. A converter that regulates nicely at light load sags under full load for this reason alone, before any resistance is considered. Note also the ordering of the work: find $\mu$ from the $\cos$ relation, find $\Delta V$ from the separate drop formula. They are two different results from the same physics, and the exam usually asks for both.

A single-phase fully controlled converter supplies a constant current load of 20 A, driven from a 50 Hz square source of amplitude 300 V, with source inductance 2 mH, at $\alpha = 30°$.

Checkpoint

Why does a larger firing angle make the overlap angle bigger, even though the load current is unchanged?

Because the commutating voltage — the voltage available to push current from the outgoing device into the incoming one — is the instantaneous supply voltage at the moment of handover, and the $\cos\alpha$ relation makes that smaller as $\alpha$ approaches $90°$. Less push, same current to transfer, so the transfer takes longer. The formula shows it: with $\cos\alpha$ small, subtracting a fixed amount from it moves the angle much further.

DC–DC Converters I — The Three Topologies

Three circuits, one method. Draw the two sub-circuits, set the volt-seconds equal, read off the answer. Once you can do that, buck, boost and buck-boost all fall out in a line and a half each.

6.1 Why not just use a resistor

You have 24 V and you want 6 V. The obvious answer is a potential divider, and it is a terrible one: three quarters of the energy goes into heating a resistor, and the output sags the moment the load changes. A linear regulator is the same idea made respectable — a transistor acting as a controlled resistor — and it has the same problem. Dropping 18 V at 2 A means burning 36 W.

The switched converter avoids this by never operating in between. The switch is either fully on, carrying current with no voltage across it, or fully off, holding voltage with no current through it. Either way the product $vi$ is zero and nothing is dissipated. The energy still has to get from input to output, and it does so in packets, buffered by an inductor and a capacitor.

That is the whole idea. The three topologies differ only in where the inductor sits.

6.2 The buck converter

Put a switch between the source and an $L$–$C$ filter, and a diode across the filter input to catch the inductor current when the switch opens.

The method, once — then applied three times
  1. Draw the circuit with the switch on. Work out $v_L$.
  2. Draw it with the switch off. The inductor current must keep flowing, so find which diode it forces into conduction. Work out $v_L$ again.
  3. Apply volt-second balance: $\;v_{L,\text{on}}\cdot DT_s + v_{L,\text{off}}\cdot(1-D)T_s = 0$.
  4. Solve for $V_o$. Done.
Why it happens — the two inductor voltages

Switch ON ($DT_s$). The source is connected to the inductor's left end, the output to its right end, so $\;v_L = V_{in} - V_o$. This is positive, so the current ramps up at $\dfrac{di_L}{dt} = \dfrac{V_{in}-V_o}{L}$. The diode is reverse-biased and blocks $V_{in}$.

Switch OFF ($(1-D)T_s$). The source is disconnected. The inductor current cannot stop, so it forward-biases the diode and circulates through it. The inductor's left end is now clamped to zero volts, so $\;v_L = -V_o$ and the current ramps down at $\dfrac{-V_o}{L}$.

Now set the volt-seconds equal. Positive area during $DT_s$, negative area during $(1-D)T_s$:

$$(V_{in} - V_o)\,D T_s \;+\; (-V_o)(1-D)T_s = 0$$ $$V_{in}D - V_oD - V_o + V_oD = 0$$
Key result — the buck converter
$$\boxed{\,V_o = D\,V_{in}\,}\qquad 0 \le D \le 1 \;\Rightarrow\; V_o \le V_{in}$$

It can only step down — hence "buck". The input current is $I_{in} = D\,I_o$: less current in than out, because less voltage out than in. Power balances.

6.2.1  The waveform stack

This figure is the one to be able to draw from memory. Every quantity you will ever be asked to size — ripple, RMS current, voltage rating — is read off it.

Watch out

$i_C$ is symmetric about zero and $i_L$ is not. Students routinely draw $i_C$ with a DC offset, which would mean the capacitor gains charge every cycle — impossible in steady state (that is charge balance, from Chapter 1). The capacitor takes the ripple; the load takes the average.

6.3 The boost converter

Move the inductor to the input side, put the switch across the line to ground and the diode in series with the output, and the same analysis gives you a converter that steps up. Figure 6.3 draws it next to its two switch states, so you can see exactly which components are carrying current in each.

Why it happens — storing then stacking

Switch ON. The inductor is connected straight across the source with its other end grounded, so $v_L = V_{in}$ and the current ramps up, storing energy. The diode is reverse-biased; the capacitor alone supplies the load.

Switch OFF. The inductor current must continue, so it forces the diode on and flows into the output. Now the inductor's right end sits at $V_o$, so $v_L = V_{in} - V_o$, which is negative because $V_o > V_{in}$.

The trick in one sentence: the inductor's voltage adds to the source during the off time, so the output must exceed the input.

$$V_{in}\,DT_s + (V_{in} - V_o)(1-D)T_s = 0$$
Key result — the boost converter
$$\boxed{\,V_o = \frac{V_{in}}{1-D}\,}\qquad I_{in} = \frac{I_o}{1-D}$$

At $D = 0$, $V_o = V_{in}$; at $D = 0.75$, $V_o = 4V_{in}$. The gain runs away as $D \to 1$ — which is why real boost converters are not operated above about $D = 0.8$. The switch and the diode both have to block the full $V_o$.

Exam note — the boost is the examined topology

Quiz Q8 (6 marks) and Tutorial Q11 are the same boost design problem with different numbers — both ask for $L$, $C$ and the switch RMS current, and both phrase the ripple spec as "peak to peak output voltage ripple is 5% of nominal value". Chapter 7 works both in full. If you prepare one DC–DC topology properly, prepare this one.

6.4 The buck-boost converter

Put the switch in series with the source, the inductor in shunt, and the diode in series with the output. Now the inductor is never connected to the source and the load at the same time — it charges from one and discharges into the other.

Switch ON: $v_L = V_{in}$ (the inductor is straight across the source). Switch OFF: $v_L = -V_o$ (it is straight across the output). Figure 6.5 shows why those two statements are true by drawing the circuit each state collapses to.

$$V_{in}\,DT_s + (-V_o)(1-D)T_s = 0$$
Key result — the buck-boost converter
$$\boxed{\,|V_o| = \frac{D}{1-D}V_{in}\,}\qquad\textbf{with the output polarity reversed}$$

Below $D = 0.5$ it steps down; above $D = 0.5$ it steps up. The output is negative with respect to the input ground — that is not an error, it is forced by the inductor discharging in the direction it was charged. The lecture notes state this explicitly in the same breath as the result, so it is clearly examinable.

The inductor here carries $I_{L,avg} = I_{in} + I_o$ — not $I_o$ as in the buck, not $I_{in}$ as in the boost. It handles all the transferred energy, which is why buck-boost inductors are the largest of the three.

Checkpoint

A buck-boost runs at $D = 0.5$. What is $|V_o|$, and what is special about this operating point?

$|V_o| = \frac{0.5}{0.5}V_{in} = V_{in}$. It is the crossover: below $D=0.5$ the converter bucks, above it boosts. It is the only duty ratio at which a buck-boost is a unity-gain inverter.

6.6 Adding isolation: the flyback

Replace the buck-boost's inductor with a two-winding version — primary $N_p$, secondary $N_s$, dots opposing — and you have a flyback. Energy is stored in the core while the switch is on, and delivered to the secondary when it opens. The circuit is a buck-boost with a turns ratio and galvanic isolation.

The volt-second balance is the same, with the output referred to the primary through $N_p/N_s$:

$$V_{in}\,DT_s + \left(-\frac{N_p}{N_s}V_o\right)(1-D)T_s = 0$$
Key result — the flyback
$$V_o = n\,V_{in}\frac{D}{1-D},\qquad n = \frac{N_s}{N_p}$$

The buck-boost formula with $n$ bolted on. Set $n = 1$ and you recover it exactly.

Correction — the flyback is not the only one

An earlier version of this chapter said the flyback was the only isolated topology in your material. That was wrong. The tablet lecture notes develop the forward converter, the push-pull, the full bridge and the phase-shifted full bridge, with circuits, volt-second balance and the core-flux argument that distinguishes them. §§6.7–6.9 cover all of them, and they are examinable.

6.7 The forward converter

The flyback stores energy in the core and releases it when the switch opens. The forward converter does the opposite: it transfers energy while the switch is on, straight through the transformer, and the core's magnetising current is a nuisance to be dealt with rather than the mechanism. That one difference drives everything else about it — including the odd-looking extra winding.

Key result — the forward converter

With the dots aiding, the secondary presents $nV_{in}$ while the switch is on and the diode $D_2$ freewheels the inductor while it is off:

$$\text{on: } v_L = nV_{in} - V_o \qquad \text{off: } v_L = -V_o$$ $$\boxed{\,V_o = n\,V_{in}\,D\,}\qquad n = \frac{n_s}{n_p}$$

This is a buck with a turns ratio — set $n = 1$ and it is exactly $V_o = DV_{in}$. Compare the flyback, which is a buck-boost with a turns ratio. That pairing is the cleanest way to remember both.

6.8 The push-pull converter, and why the core is the point

The forward converter drives its core in one direction only. The flux ramps up while the switch conducts and is reset to zero by the reset leg — it never goes negative. Half the available flux swing is therefore wasted, and the core has to be twice the size it would need to be.

The push-pull fixes exactly this. Two switches drive a centre-tapped primary in opposite directions on alternate half-cycles, so the flux swings both ways.

Key result — the push-pull

Each switch is on for $DT_s$, with $D < 0.5$ so they never overlap. Over a full period there are two conducting intervals and one freewheeling interval of $(1-2D)T_s$:

$$2DT_s\,(nV_{in} - V_o) + (1-2D)T_s\,(-V_o) = 0$$ $$\boxed{\,V_o = 2\,n\,V_{in}\,D\,}\qquad 0 < D < 0.5$$

Twice the forward converter's output for the same $n$ and $D$ — because the output is fed twice per period instead of once. The factor of two and the $D < 0.5$ limit come as a pair: at $D = 0.5$ both switches would be on at once and short the supply through the primary.

6.9 The full bridge, and why frequency buys size

Replace the centre-tapped primary with a full H-bridge and you get the same both-sided core utilisation without needing a centre tap, at the cost of two more switches. The volt-second balance is identical, so the result is too: $V_o = 2nV_{in}D$.

Key result — why any of this is worth the extra devices

Your notes box the reason, and it is the single most useful sentence in this part of the course:

$$V = N\,\Delta B\,A\,f$$

For a required voltage, the core's cross-sectional area $A$ is inversely proportional to the switching frequency $f$. Double the frequency and you can halve the core. That is why isolated converters run at tens or hundreds of kilohertz while a mains transformer at 50 Hz is a lump of iron — and it is why the switching losses of §2.5 are worth paying.

Both-sided core utilisation doubles $\Delta B$ for free, which is the same saving again. Forward → push-pull → bridge is a sequence of ways to use more of the core.

6.10 The four-switch buck-boost

One more converter from your notes, and it is not isolated — it solves the buck-boost's other problem. The §6.4 buck-boost inverts its output, which is often unusable. Put the inductor on the input side and an H-bridge after it, and you get a buck-boost whose output is the right way up.

Key result — the four-switch buck-boost
$$\text{on: } v_L = V_{in} - V_o \qquad \text{off: } v_L = V_{in} + V_o$$ $$DT_s(V_{in}-V_o) + (1-D)T_s(V_{in}+V_o) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (1-2D)V_o + V_{in} = 0$$ $$\boxed{\,V_o = \frac{V_{in}}{2D-1}\,}$$

At $D = 0.75$, $V_o = 2V_{in}$; at $D = 0.6$, $V_o = 5V_{in}$. The output is positive and the gain is continuous through step-down and step-up.

Watch out — $D < 0.5$ is forbidden, and the notes say why

Below $D = 0.5$ the formula returns a negative $V_o$. That is not a useful inverting mode: a negative output forward-biases the body diodes of the bridge switches, which short-circuits the output. Your notes draw the chain explicitly — $D < 0.5$ → forward bias of body diode → short circuit of output voltage → avoid. Operate this converter only above $D = 0.5$.

DC–DC Converters II — Sizing, Ripple and Currents

Chapter 6 told you what $V_o$ will be. This one tells you how big the inductor has to be, how big the capacitor, and how much current the devices carry — which is the part the exam actually asks for numbers on.

7.1 Sizing the inductor from the ripple you will tolerate

The inductor's value does not set the output voltage — Chapter 6 showed that $V_o$ depends only on $D$. What $L$ sets is the ripple: how much the current wanders either side of its average during one switching cycle.

And finding it needs no new physics. Over whichever sub-interval you like, the inductor voltage is constant, so the current is a straight ramp:

$$v_L = L\frac{di_L}{dt} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta i_L = \frac{v_L \times \Delta t}{L}$$

Pick the easier of the two sub-intervals, rearrange for $L$, and you are done.

Why it happens — choose the sub-interval that has the fewest unknowns

For the buck, the off interval is cleanest: $v_L = -V_o$ for a time $(1-D)T_s$, so $\;V_o(1-D)T_s = L\,\Delta i_L$.

For the boost, the on interval is cleanest: $v_L = V_{in}$ for a time $DT_s$, so $\;V_{in}DT_s = L\,\Delta i_L$.

Either interval gives the same answer — they must, because the ripple is the same triangle seen from both ends. Use whichever one has the quantity you were given.

Key result — inductor sizing
Converter$L$From which interval
Buck$\dfrac{V_o(1-D)}{\Delta i_L\,f_s}$off, $v_L = -V_o$
Boost$\dfrac{V_{in}\,D}{\Delta i_L\,f_s}$on, $v_L = V_{in}$

$\Delta i_L$ is the peak-to-peak ripple. Design practice from the lecture notes: take it as 5–10% of the relevant average current — $I_o$ for the buck, $I_{in}$ for the boost. $f_s$ is the designer's choice.

7.2 Sizing the capacitor — and why the buck is different

The capacitor's job is to hold the output voltage steady while the current into the output node fluctuates. How much capacitance you need depends on how much charge it has to absorb or supply between refills — and that differs sharply between the buck and the other two.

7.2.1  The buck: only the ripple reaches the capacitor

In a buck the inductor feeds the output node all the time. The load takes the average, so the capacitor only ever sees the triangular ripple $i_C = i_L - I_o$. Charge flows in for half the cycle and out for the other half.

The charge delivered in one half-cycle is the area of the positive triangle: base $T_s/2$, height $\Delta i_L/2$, so

$$\Delta Q = \tfrac12 \times \frac{\Delta i_L}{2} \times \frac{T_s}{2} = \frac{\Delta i_L T_s}{8}$$
Key result — buck capacitor
$$C = \frac{\Delta i_L}{8\,\Delta V_o\,f_s}$$

That $\tfrac18$ is not a fudge factor — it is $\tfrac12$ (triangle) $\times\ \tfrac12$ (half the ripple) $\times\ \tfrac12$ (half the period). Being able to say where it comes from is worth a mark.

7.2.2  The boost and buck-boost: the capacitor carries the whole load

Now the difference. In both of these, the diode is reverse-biased for the entire on interval $DT_s$ — the output is completely disconnected from the rest of the circuit. During that time the capacitor supplies the load by itself.

$$\Delta Q = I_o \times D T_s$$
Key result — boost and buck-boost capacitor
$$C = \frac{I_o\,D}{\Delta V_o\,f_s}$$

$\Delta V_o$ is the peak-to-peak output ripple, taken as 5–10% of $V_o$ in the lecture notes' design practice.

Watch out — do not use the buck's $\tfrac18$ formula on a boost

They differ by far more than the numeral. The boost capacitor must supply the full load current for $DT_s$; the buck capacitor only mops up ripple. For the same specification the boost needs a much larger capacitor, and using $\Delta i_L/(8\Delta V_o f_s)$ there will undersize it badly.

One-line test: ask whether the output is ever disconnected. Buck — never, so ripple only. Boost and buck-boost — yes, for $DT_s$, so the whole load current.

7.3 Device currents — average and RMS

This is the one place the examiner asks you to integrate rather than substitute, and it is the same operation as §3.6: a device carries current for part of the period and nothing for the rest, and the averaging window is always the whole period $T_s$.

With the standard assumption that the ripple is small — which every one of these problems makes — the current during conduction is flat at its average value, and the integrals become trivial:

$$I_{avg} = \frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{DT_s}\!\! I\,dt = D\,I \qquad\qquad I_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_s}\int_0^{DT_s}\!\! I^2\,dt} = I\sqrt{D}$$

Checkpoint

In a boost converter, why is $I_{D,avg}$ exactly $I_o$ rather than something involving $D$?

Because the diode is the only path from the converter into the output node. Everything the load consumes must pass through it, so its average must equal the load's average. The algebra agrees: $I_{in}(1-D) = \frac{I_o}{1-D}(1-D) = I_o$. When a formula collapses this neatly there is usually a conservation argument behind it — and quoting that argument is faster than the algebra in an exam.

7.4 Continuous or discontinuous conduction

Everything so far assumed the inductor current never reaches zero. Lighten the load far enough and it will — the diode stops conducting partway through the off interval, the current sits at zero for a while, and the converter enters discontinuous conduction mode (DCM).

Why it happens — turning the boundary into a design rule

At the boundary, $I_o = \Delta i_L/2$. For a buck, $I_o = V_o/R_L$ and $\Delta i_L = V_o(1-D)T_s/L$ (§7.1 rearranged). Substituting:

$$\frac{V_o}{R_L} = \frac{V_o(1-D)T_s}{2L} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{2L}{R_L T_s} = 1-D$$

The left side is a dimensionless group the notes call $K$. The right side is its critical value.

Key result — the CCM/DCM criterion
$$K = \frac{2L}{R_L T_s}\qquad \begin{cases} K > K_{crit} & \text{CCM}\\ K < K_{crit} & \text{DCM}\end{cases}$$
Converter$K_{crit}$
Buck$1-D$
Boost$D(1-D)^2$
Buck-boost$(1-D)^2$

$K$ is big when $L$ is big and the load is heavy (small $R_L$) — both of which keep you in CCM, exactly as intuition says.

In DCM the conversion ratio is no longer $D$. The inductor voltage is zero for the dead interval, so volt-second balance has three terms and the algebra ends in a quadratic. The lecture notes carry it through for the buck:

$$(V_{in}-V_o)D_1T_s + (-V_o)D_2T_s + 0\cdot D_3T_s = 0,\qquad D_1+D_2+D_3 = 1$$
Key result — buck in DCM
$$\frac{V_o}{V_{in}} = \frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1 + 4K/D_1^{\,2}}}$$

where $D_1$ is the fraction of the period for which the switch is on. Set $K = K_{crit} = 1-D$ and this collapses back to $V_o/V_{in} = D$ — always worth checking.

7.4.1  The same derivation for the boost and the buck-boost

The tablet notes stop after the buck, but the full-course notebook carries both other ratios in its summary table. They are derived here anyway, for two reasons: the table gives results with no working, and "derive the DCM ratio for a boost" is a question the method makes routine and memory makes impossible. The derivations below agree with that table exactly, which is the best check either of them could have.

Procedure — DCM conversion ratio, any of the three
  1. Volt-second balance over the three intervals. The dead interval $D_3$ contributes nothing, so only $D_1$ and $D_2$ appear.
  2. Charge balance on the output. The diode current is a triangle of peak $I_{pk} = V_{in}D_1T_s/L$ occupying only $D_2$ of the period, so its average is $\tfrac12 I_{pk}D_2$, and that must equal $I_o = V_o/R$.
  3. Eliminate $D_2$ between the two, and write the answer in terms of $K = 2L/(RT_s)$.

Boost. Step 1 gives $V_{in}D_1 + (V_{in}-V_o)D_2 = 0$, so with $M = V_o/V_{in}$, $\;D_2 = D_1/(M-1)$. Step 2 gives $D_1D_2 = KM$. Eliminating $D_2$,

$$\frac{D_1^{\,2}}{M-1} = KM \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^2 - M - \frac{D_1^{\,2}}{K} = 0$$
Key result — boost in DCM
$$\boxed{\;\frac{V_o}{V_{in}} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 4D_1^{\,2}/K}}{2}\;}$$

Check it: at the boundary $K = K_{crit} = D(1-D)^2$ this gives exactly $1/(1-D)$, the CCM answer. Unlike the buck, the boost's DCM ratio rises as the load lightens — $K$ falls, the square root grows, and $V_o$ runs away. An unloaded boost converter in DCM will overshoot badly, which is why one is never left open-circuit without feedback.

Buck-boost. Simpler, because the inductor sees the input and the output separately. Step 1: $V_{in}D_1 = |V_o|D_2$, so $D_2 = D_1/M$. Step 2 is unchanged, $D_1D_2 = KM$. Eliminating $D_2$ gives $D_1^{\,2} = KM^2$:

Key result — buck-boost in DCM
$$\boxed{\;\frac{|V_o|}{V_{in}} = \frac{D_1}{\sqrt{K}}\;}$$

The cleanest of the three. At $K = K_{crit} = (1-D)^2$ it collapses to $D/(1-D)$ — the CCM result — as it must. Note what it says: in DCM the buck-boost's gain depends on the load through $K$, not on duty ratio alone. That is the general lesson of DCM and the reason converters are designed to stay out of it: in CCM the conversion ratio is a function of $D$ only; in DCM it is a function of $D$, $L$, $R$ and $f_{sw}$ together.

Worked example — the same converter, both sides of the boundary

A buck-boost runs at $D = 0.5$, $f_{sw} = 50$ kHz, $L = 100$ µH, $V_{in} = 12$ V. Find $V_o$ for $R = 5\;\Omega$ and for $R = 50\;\Omega$.

Step 1 — which mode? $K_{crit} = (1-D)^2 = 0.25$.

$$K = \frac{2L}{RT_s} = \frac{2(100\times10^{-6})(50\times10^{3})}{R} = \frac{10}{R}$$

At $R = 5\;\Omega$: $K = 2.0 > 0.25$, so CCM. At $R = 50\;\Omega$: $K = 0.2 < 0.25$, so DCM.

Step 2 — apply the right formula.

$$R = 5\;\Omega:\quad |V_o| = \frac{D}{1-D}V_{in} = \frac{0.5}{0.5}(12) = 12\ \text{V}$$ $$R = 50\;\Omega:\quad |V_o| = \frac{D_1}{\sqrt{K}}V_{in} = \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{0.2}}(12) = \frac{0.5}{0.4472}(12) = 13.4\ \text{V}$$
the same converter, the same duty ratio, and the output has moved by 12%. That is the whole reason DCM matters: a control loop tuned for the CCM gain finds the plant gain has changed underneath it when the load drops. It also shows the direction — lightening the load raises the output in DCM, for the boost and the buck-boost both.
Watch out — the coverage frontier

The DCM derivation is carried through only for the buck in the lecture notes. The boost DCM page is a heading and a circuit with nothing under it, and buck-boost DCM is absent. The $K_{crit}$ values above for boost and buck-boost are in the notes — written out as $K_{crit} = D(1-D)^2$ and $(1-D)^2$ with their critical load resistances — and so are the two conversion ratios, in the full-course notebook's DCM summary, quoted without working. §7.4.1 supplies the working. Each result is checked twice: against the notebook's quoted form, and against the CCM result at the boundary. If one is asked for, derive it the same way — three volt-second terms, $D_1+D_2+D_3=1$, then eliminate $D_2$ using the area of the current triangle — rather than trying to recall a formula you never saw.

7.5 The design procedure, and the question it answers

Procedure — any DC–DC design question
  1. $D$ from the voltage ratio. Buck $D = V_o/V_{in}$; boost $D = 1 - V_{in}/V_o$; buck-boost $D = |V_o|/(V_{in}+|V_o|)$.
  2. The currents. $I_o = P/V_o$ and $I_{in} = P/V_{in}$ if power is given; otherwise $I_o = V_o/R_L$ and then $I_{in}$ from the topology.
  3. Turn the percentage specs into absolute numbers. "Ripple 10% of the average" means $\Delta i_L = 0.1 \times$ (the relevant current); "5% of nominal" means $\Delta V_o = 0.05\,V_o$. Do this explicitly — it is where slips happen.
  4. $L$ from §7.1, $C$ from §7.2 — choosing the right one of the two capacitor formulas.
  5. Device currents from the §7.3 table. Remember which current the inductor carries in this topology.
  6. Sanity-check the magnitudes. $L$ in the hundreds of µH and $C$ in the tens of µF are typical at tens of kHz. A millihenry at 20 kHz should make you re-check your decimal point — see the next box.
Worked example 7.1 — Quiz Q8, 6 marks, worked by the examiner himself

A boost converter has $V_{in} = 25$ V, $V_o = 100$ V, $f_{sw} = 20$ kHz, output power 500 W, allowable peak-to-peak output voltage ripple 5% of nominal, allowable input peak-to-peak current ripple 10% of its average. Find (a) $L$, (b) $C$, (c) the RMS current through switch S.

Step 1 — duty ratio.

$$100 = \frac{25}{1-D} \;\Rightarrow\; 1-D = 0.25 \;\Rightarrow\; D = 0.75$$

Step 2 — currents and the specs in absolute terms. $T_s = 1/20\,\text{kHz} = 50\ \mu$s.

$$I_{in} = \frac{P}{V_{in}} = \frac{500}{25} = 20\ \text{A} \qquad \Delta i_L = 0.10 \times 20 = 2\ \text{A}$$ $$I_o = \frac{P}{V_o} = \frac{500}{100} = 5\ \text{A} \qquad \Delta V_o = 0.05 \times 100 = 5\ \text{V}$$

(a) Inductance.

$$L = \frac{V_{in}D}{\Delta i_L f_s} = \frac{25 \times 0.75}{2 \times 20000} = \frac{18.75}{40000} = \mathbf{468.75\ \mu H}$$

(b) Capacitance. Boost, so the capacitor carries the full load current during $DT_s$:

$$C = \frac{I_o D}{\Delta V_o f_s} = \frac{5 \times 0.75}{5 \times 20000} = \frac{3.75}{100000} = \mathbf{37.5\ \mu F}$$

(c) Switch RMS current. The switch carries the inductor current $I_{in}$ for $DT_s$:

$$I_{S,rms} = I_{in}\sqrt{D} = 20\sqrt{0.75} = 20 \times 0.8660 = \mathbf{17.32\ A}$$

Note what the switch rating has to be: 17.3 A RMS while blocking $V_o = 100$ V — and the diode must block the same 100 V, not the 25 V input. In a boost, both devices are rated for the output, which is the price of stepping up. Note also that $\Delta i_L$ is a percentage of $I_{in}$, not of $I_o$ — the ripple spec always refers to the current the inductor carries, and in a boost that is the input current.

Watch out — there is an error in the model answer you were given

The examiner worked this question in red ink on the quiz. His method lines are correct and worth copying exactly. But his answer to part (a) is written as "46.875 mH", and the correct value from his own fraction is 468.75 µH = 0.46875 mH — a slip of a factor of 100.

Work it yourself: $\frac{25 \times 0.75 \times 50\times10^{-6}}{2} = \frac{937.5\times10^{-6}}{2} = 468.75\times10^{-6}$ H.

Learn his method — $V_{in} = L\,\Delta i/(D T_s)$ is exactly right — and use your own arithmetic. And treat this as the reason step 6 of the procedure exists: 46 mH at 20 kHz would be an absurdly large inductor for a 500 W converter, and noticing that is what catches the error.

Inverters I — Single-Phase

A rectifier takes the AC the grid gives you and averages it down. An inverter does the opposite and harder thing: it starts with flat DC and manufactures an alternating waveform that was never there.

8.1 You cannot make a sine from a switch

Here is the problem stated honestly. You have a DC source and some switches. A switch gives you two voltages — connected, or not. There is no setting marked "0.7 of the way". So you cannot produce a sine wave; the best you can do is produce a waveform whose average over each short interval traces one out, and then lean on the load's inductance to smooth the rest.

The crudest version of that idea is to flip the polarity once per half-cycle. The result is a square wave — nothing like a sine to look at, but it has a sinusoidal fundamental at the frequency you chose, and that fundamental is what a motor responds to. Everything else is harmonics, and Chapter 10 is about pushing them somewhere harmless.

8.2 The half-bridge inverter

Split the DC supply into two halves with a mid-point $o$ — the lecture notes call it the "fictitious mid point", and in practice it is made with two capacitors. One leg of two switches, $S_1$ to the upper rail and $S_2$ to the lower, with the load from the leg's mid-point $A$ back to $o$. Figure 8.1 is that circuit.

Drive $S_1$ and $S_2$ complementarily at 50% duty:

Watch out — the one rule you never break

The lecture notes put it bluntly in the margin: "S1, S2 are complementary, otherwise DC will be short and huge current will flow." Turning both devices in a leg on at once puts a short straight across the DC link. This is called shoot-through and it destroys the devices. Real inverters insert a deliberate dead time — a few microseconds with both off — to guarantee it cannot happen.

8.2.1  The Fourier series, derived

A square wave of amplitude $V_{DC}/2$, odd about the origin. Odd symmetry kills the DC term and all the cosine terms at once, so $a_0 = 0$ and $a_n = 0$, and only $b_n$ survives:

$$b_n = \frac{2}{T}\left[\int_0^{T/2}\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin(n\omega t)\,dt - \int_{T/2}^{T}\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin(n\omega t)\,dt\right]$$

Carrying out both integrals and collecting terms gives

$$b_n = \frac{V_{DC}}{n\pi}\big(1 - \cos n\pi\big)$$
Key result — half-bridge output
$$b_n = \begin{cases} \dfrac{2V_{DC}}{n\pi}, & n \text{ odd}\\[6pt] 0, & n \text{ even}\end{cases} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{ao} = \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}\frac{2V_{DC}}{n\pi}\sin(n\omega t)$$

The fundamental has peak amplitude $\dfrac{2V_{DC}}{\pi} = 0.637\,V_{DC}$. Harmonic amplitudes fall off as $1/n$.

Why it happens — $(1-\cos n\pi)$ is a parity switch

$\cos n\pi$ is $-1$ for odd $n$ and $+1$ for even $n$. So the bracket is $2$ for odd harmonics and exactly $0$ for even ones. The underlying reason is half-wave symmetry: the second half-cycle is the exact negative of the first, and any waveform with that property contains only odd harmonics. You will meet the same argument again in §9.4, where it kills the triplens.

Why it happens — what the switches must be able to do

The notes put the requirement precisely: "Switches have to block +ve voltage but current should be allowed both ways because we don't know the type of load."

With an inductive load, current lags voltage, so there are intervals where $v_{ao}$ has already flipped but the current has not. During those intervals the current must flow against the switch. Hence every switch gets an anti-parallel diode — a MOSFET's body diode, or an explicit one across an IGBT or thyristor. Those diodes are what return the load's stored energy to the DC link, which is why they are called feedback diodes.

Checkpoint

A half-bridge and a full bridge run from the same 400 V DC link. What fundamental RMS output voltage does each give?

Three legs, six devices, six states. Get the conduction table right and every waveform, every voltage level and the entire harmonic analysis follows from it mechanically.

9.1 Three legs of the same thing

A three-phase inverter is three half-bridge legs across one DC link, their mid-points $a$, $b$, $c$ feeding a star-connected load whose neutral is $o$. Each leg has an upper and a lower device, strictly complementary:

9.2 The 180° conduction mode

Each device conducts for $180°$ — half the cycle — and the three legs are displaced by $120°$. Since the two devices in a leg are complementary, exactly three devices conduct at any instant: one from each leg.

9.2.1  Where the thirds come from

The voltage levels $\pm V_{DC}/3$ and $\pm 2V_{DC}/3$ are not arbitrary — they fall straight out of the resistor network in each step.

Why it happens — two in parallel, one in series

Take step 1 (T5, T6, T1). Nodes $a$ and $c$ are tied to the positive rail; node $b$ is tied to the negative rail. The load is three equal impedances $Z$ from $a$, $b$, $c$ to the star point $o$.

So two arms ($a$ and $c$) sit in parallel — $Z/2$ — and that combination is in series with the third arm ($b$), giving a total of $3Z/2$ across $V_{DC}$:

$$I = \frac{V_{DC}}{3Z/2} = \frac{2V_{DC}}{3Z}$$ $$v_{ao} = v_{co} = \frac{Z}{2}\cdot I = \frac{V_{DC}}{3}, \qquad |v_{bo}| = Z\cdot I = \frac{2V_{DC}}{3}$$

The arm that is alone on its rail gets $\frac23 V_{DC}$; the two sharing get $\frac13$ each. Every step is that same division, with the roles rotated.

9.3 Pole, phase and common-mode voltages

There are two different "neutrals" in this circuit and confusing them is the standard way to lose marks. $N$ is the DC link's mid-point; $o$ is the load's star point. They are not at the same potential.

$$v_{ao} = v_{aN} - v_{oN}$$

$v_{aN}$ is the pole voltage — what the leg does, always $\pm V_{DC}/2$. $v_{ao}$ is the phase voltage — what the load actually sees. The difference is $v_{oN}$, the common-mode voltage, and it is the average of the three pole voltages:

$$v_{oN} = \frac{v_{aN} + v_{bN} + v_{cN}}{3}$$
Key result — the common-mode voltage is a third harmonic

With three legs each at $\pm V_{DC}/2$, the sum $v_{aN}+v_{bN}+v_{cN}$ is $\pm V_{DC}/2$ (two of one sign, one of the other), so

$$v_{oN} = \pm\frac{V_{DC}}{6}$$

and it alternates at three times the fundamental frequency. The star point of the load bounces up and down at the third harmonic relative to the DC link mid-point.

Why it happens — and why the load never notices

The third harmonic in $v_{oN}$ is common to all three phases: it appears identically at $a$, $b$ and $c$. Subtract one phase from another to get a line voltage and it cancels exactly. With no neutral connection it cannot drive any current either.

So the triplen harmonics are generated, they are measurable at the star point, and they do no harm. This is the same fact as §3.4.2 seen from the other side — there the triplens vanished from the rectifier's line current, here they vanish from the inverter's line voltage, and for the same reason: they are in phase across all three phases, and a three-wire system has nowhere to put them.

9.4 Harmonic content

The phase voltage $v_{ao}$ has quarter-wave symmetry, so $a_n = 0$ and all even $b_n$ vanish. For the odd ones, integrating the staircase gives the same clean $1/n$ family as the single-phase case:

$$v_{ao,n} = \frac{2V_{DC}}{n\pi}$$

And the third harmonic? The lecture notes do it as a two-part integral across the staircase levels and get $b_3 = 0$ exactly. The surviving orders are

Key result — which harmonics survive
$$n = 6k \pm 1 = 1,\;5,\;7,\;11,\;13,\;\dots$$

No even harmonics (half-wave symmetry), no triplens (three-phase symmetry). The lowest unwanted component is the fifth, at five times the fundamental — which is already a real improvement over the single-phase inverter, where the third is the nearest neighbour.

This is the same $6k\pm1$ family as the three-phase rectifier's line current in §3.4.2. The number six is the pulse number in both cases, and it is not a coincidence: six states per cycle produces sixth-order structure.

Watch out — the low-order harmonics are the expensive ones

The notes are explicit about why this matters: the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th in the phase voltages drive harmonic currents in the motor, which produce extra conduction losses and parasitic torques but no useful output. They are too close to the fundamental to filter cheaply. That problem is the entire motivation for Chapter 10.

Checkpoint

A six-step inverter runs from a 600 V DC link. What is the RMS of the fundamental line-to-line voltage?

The line voltage is a quasi-square wave of amplitude $V_{DC}$ with $120°$ conduction. Using the result from §3.4.2 for a $120°$ block, $V_{1,pk} = \frac{4V_{DC}}{\pi}\sin 60° = \frac{2\sqrt3 V_{DC}}{\pi} = 1.103 V_{DC}$, so $V_{1,rms} = 1.103(600)/\sqrt2 = 468$ V. Worth remembering as a sanity figure: a six-step inverter gives about 0.78 $V_{DC}$ RMS line-to-line, so a 600 V link supports roughly a 400 V motor.

9.5 The 120° conduction mode

The other way to run the same six switches. Hold each device on for $120°$ instead of $180°$, and the arithmetic of the previous four sections runs again with one number changed — but the answer comes out inverted, and that inversion is the whole reason the mode is worth knowing.

Why it happens — only two devices on at once

Six devices, each on for $120°$ of $360°$, gives $6 \times 120/360 = 2$ devices conducting at any instant instead of three. Two conducting means one phase of the load is open-circuited at all times, and each phase spends $60°$ open in each half-cycle.

An open phase carries no current, so it develops no voltage across the load impedance: its phase voltage is zero for those $60°$, not some intermediate step. That single fact is what changes the shape.

Key result — 120° is 180° with phase and line swapped

In $180°$ mode the phase voltage is the six-step and the line voltage is the quasi-square. In $120°$ mode it is the other way round.

$$v_{ao}\ \text{(phase)} = \pm\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\ \text{for } 120°,\ 0\ \text{for } 60° \qquad v_{ab}\ \text{(line)} = \text{six-step at } \pm V_{DC},\ \pm\frac{V_{DC}}{2}$$

Everything else follows from that swap. Note the cost while you are here: the peak phase voltage drops from $2V_{DC}/3$ in §9.2 to $V_{DC}/2$ here, and the fundamental drops with it.

The fundamental is the number that decides between the two modes. For a quasi-square wave of amplitude $A$ conducting for $2\delta$, the fundamental peak is $\frac{4A}{\pi}\sin\delta$. Here $A = V_{DC}/2$ and $2\delta = 120°$, so

$$\hat{V}_{ao,1} = \frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\cdot\sin 60° = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}V_{DC} \approx 0.551\,V_{DC}$$

against $2V_{DC}/\pi \approx 0.637\,V_{DC}$ for the six-step of §9.2. The ratio is exactly $\sqrt{3}/2 = 0.866$: 120° mode gives 86.6% of the fundamental that 180° mode gives from the same DC link.

Exam note — what to say if asked to compare

Three things carry the marks, in this order:

  1. Two devices conduct, not three, and one phase is always open.
  2. Phase and line waveforms swap shapes — quasi-square phase, six-step line.
  3. The fundamental is $\sqrt{3}/2$ of the 180° value, which is why 180° is the mode actually used when output is what you want.

And the restriction, which the notebook boxes and circles: $180°$ mode works into any load; $120°$ mode is analysed for a resistive load only. The reason is the open phase. With a resistive load no current wants to flow in it, so its voltage is genuinely zero. With an inductive load the current in the phase being switched out cannot stop instantly — it commutates into that leg's antiparallel diode, which clamps the "open" phase to a rail and destroys the clean three-level shape. Say this if you are asked to compare the modes; it is the difference that actually matters in a motor drive.

The compensating advantage: because a device turns off $60°$ before its partner in the same leg turns on, there is a built-in dead band and shoot-through is impossible. In 180° mode the two devices in a leg hand over instantaneously, and a real bridge must insert dead-time by hand to stop them overlapping.

Chapter 10 post-midterm · core

PWM and Harmonic Control

The six-step inverter's harmonics sit at five and seven times the fundamental — too close to filter without a huge, lossy, slow filter. Pulse-width modulation does not remove harmonics. It moves them somewhere you can afford to deal with.

10.1 The problem, stated as a filter requirement

A 50 Hz six-step inverter puts its nearest unwanted component at 250 Hz. To remove it while passing 50 Hz you need a filter with a corner somewhere around 150 Hz — and the lecture notes list exactly what that costs:

  • $L$ and $C$ values are huge at those frequencies.
  • The filter shifts the phase of the output.
  • The filter components have losses of their own.
  • Meanwhile the harmonic currents cause $I^2R$ losses in the load.

Now suppose the nearest unwanted component sat at 10 kHz instead. A filter with a 500 Hz corner would do, and at 10 kHz the load's own inductance does most of the work for free. That relocation is the whole of PWM. As the notes put it: throwing the harmonics to higher frequencies so the filtering requirements are relaxed.

10.2 How the pulses are made

Take two signals into a comparator:

  • A modulating (reference) signal $v_m$ — a sine at the frequency you want out, amplitude $V_m$.
  • A carrier $v_c$ — a triangle at a much higher frequency $f_c$, amplitude $V_c$.

Whenever the sine is above the triangle, the upper device goes on; otherwise the lower one does. The output is a train of pulses whose widths follow the sine — wide where the sine is high, narrow where it is low.

The notes record three carrier shapes, generated by three counter types: a rising sawtooth (up counter), a falling sawtooth (down counter), and a symmetric triangle (up-down counter). The symmetric triangle is the usual choice.

10.3 The modulation index, and what it buys you

Key result — amplitude control
$$m = \frac{V_m}{V_c} \qquad\qquad v_{ao,1} = m\,\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t$$

$m$ is the modulation index — the ratio of reference amplitude to carrier amplitude. The fundamental output of a leg is $m \times V_{DC}/2$ in peak amplitude, linearly, for $0 \le m \le 1$.

This is the formula the drive calculation in Chapter 11 turns on, so it is worth memorising in this exact form: peak phase fundamental $= m\,V_{DC}/2$.

Why it happens — where $m V_{DC}/2$ actually comes from

It is worth seeing this derived once, because it is three lines and it explains why the relationship is exactly linear rather than approximately so.

Take one carrier cycle, short enough that the reference is effectively constant at $V_m$. The switch is on while reference exceeds carrier. By similar triangles on the carrier ramp,

$$\frac{V_c - V_m}{\frac{T_c}{2} - t_{on}} = \frac{2V_c}{\frac{T_c}{2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D = \frac{t_{on}}{T_c/2} = \frac{V_m}{2V_c} + \frac{1}{2}$$

The pole sits at $+V_{DC}/2$ for that fraction and $-V_{DC}/2$ for the rest, so its average over the carrier cycle is

$$\bar{v}_{ao} = -\frac{V_{DC}}{2} + V_{DC}D = -\frac{V_{DC}}{2} + V_{DC}\left(\frac{V_m}{2V_c} + \frac12\right) = \frac{V_m}{2V_c}\,V_{DC}$$

The $\tfrac12$ terms cancel exactly, which is the whole trick. Now let the reference be a sinusoid, $V_m \to V_m\sin\omega t$, and write $m = V_m/V_c$:

$$v_{ao}(t) = \frac{m V_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t$$

So the local average of the pole voltage tracks the reference, scaled by $V_{DC}/2$. PWM does not approximate a sine — it reproduces one exactly, in average, cycle by cycle. Everything the modulator can and cannot do follows from the one condition this derivation needed: $V_m \le V_c$, that is $m \le 1$.

So PWM gives you a second control knob. A six-step inverter's output is fixed by $V_{DC}$ alone; with PWM you can hold $V_{DC}$ constant and vary $m$, or hold $m$ constant and vary $V_{DC}$. The notes make the design consequence explicit: with a diode front-end the DC link is fixed, so you must vary $m$; with a controlled rectifier in front you can keep $m$ constant and vary $V_{DC}$ by firing angle instead.

10.4 Where the harmonics land

This is the part the exam tested, and it is worth knowing precisely because the answer is counter-intuitive — the harmonics do not fall at multiples of the carrier.

Define the frequency modulation ratio

$$m_f = \frac{f_c}{f_1}$$

The output spectrum contains the fundamental at $f_1$, and then clusters of harmonics grouped around $m_f$, $2m_f$, $3m_f$ and so on. Within each cluster the sidebands follow a parity rule that alternates between clusters:

Key result — the sideband parity rule

around $m_f$$m_f$ itself, $m_f \pm 2,\; m_f \pm 4,\; \dots$ (even offsets, and zero counts as even) $10000$, $10000 \pm 100$, $10000 \pm 200$, …

The offsets are counted in multiples of $f_1$, so "$m_f \pm 2$" means $\pm 2f_1 = \pm100$ Hz when $f_1 = 50$ Hz.

Which of $m_f$ and $2m_f$ survive is decided by the same parity rule, and they do not behave the same way. Around $m_f$ the offsets are even, and zero is an even number — so the carrier component itself is present, and it is the largest harmonic in the whole spectrum. Around $2m_f$ the offsets are odd, so there is nothing at $2m_f$ itself. Figure 10.4, two sections on, is a computed spectrum and you can read both facts straight off it: a tall line exactly on $m_f$, and a gap exactly on $2m_f$.

Worked example 10.1 — Quiz Q3

A half-bridge voltage-source converter uses sinusoidal PWM with a carrier frequency of 10 kHz and a fundamental of 50 Hz. Which of these harmonic frequencies exist in the output: 9950, 10050, 9900, 20150 Hz?

Step 1. $m_f = \dfrac{10000}{50} = 200$.

Step 2 — the first cluster, around 10 kHz: even offsets only.

$$(200 \pm 2)\times 50 = 9900,\;10100 \qquad (200 \pm 4)\times 50 = 9800,\;10200$$

So 9900 exists. But 9950 would be $m_f - 1$ and 10050 would be $m_f + 1$ — odd offsets, which are absent from this cluster. Neither exists.

Step 3 — the second cluster, around 20 kHz: odd offsets only.

$$(400 \pm 1)\times 50 = 19950,\;20050 \qquad (400 \pm 3)\times 50 = 19850,\;\mathbf{20150}$$

So 20150 exists too.

Answers: 9900 and 20150 — both of them.

Two of the four options are correct, and on the examiner's own copy both are ticked, with his working in the margin reading "$10000 \pm 100$, $10000 \pm 200$" and "$20000 \pm 50$, $20000 \pm 150$" — exactly the two rows of the table above. The two distractors are not random: 9950 and 10050 are precisely the sidebands that the parity rule forbids. The question is not testing whether you know harmonics cluster near the carrier; it is testing whether you know which ones. Memorise the parity: even offsets around odd multiples of $m_f$, odd offsets around even multiples.

10.5 Bipolar and unipolar modulation

Everything so far has been about one leg. A full bridge has two, and there are two different ways to drive them — they produce the same fundamental and very different harmonics, which is exactly the kind of distinction that makes a short exam question.

Bipolar

One reference, compared against one carrier. Reference above carrier turns on the diagonal $S_1,S_4$ and the load sees $+V_{DC}$; otherwise $S_2,S_3$ and it sees $-V_{DC}$. The output only ever takes two values, one of each sign — hence "bipolar".

Running the §10.3 averaging argument on the bridge rather than the leg doubles everything, because the load now spans two poles instead of one:

$$\bar{v}_{ab} = -V_{DC} + 2V_{DC}D = \frac{V_m}{V_c}V_{DC} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{ab}(t) = m\,V_{DC}\sin\omega t$$

10.5.1  Unipolar

Now drive the two legs from two references, one the negative of the other, each compared against the same carrier with the ordinary half-bridge rule. Leg $a$ follows $+v_{ref}$, leg $b$ follows $-v_{ref}$.

The fundamental is unchanged — substituting $v_{bo}(t) = \tfrac{mV_{DC}}{2}\sin(\omega t + 180°) = -\tfrac{mV_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t$,

$$v_{ab}(t) = v_{ao}(t) - v_{bo}(t) = \frac{mV_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t + \frac{mV_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t = m\,V_{DC}\sin\omega t$$

Identical to bipolar. Everything that differs is in the shape.

Key result — unipolar moves the first harmonic cluster to $2m_f$

Because the output edge rate is doubled, the effective carrier seen by the load is doubled. The sideband rule becomes

$$h = i\,(2m_f) \pm j$$

with the same parity convention. The entire cluster around $m_f$ vanishes. The lowest harmonic present is near $2m_f$, twice as far from the fundamental as in bipolar, so the filter has an easier job for no change in switching device.

10.6 Over-modulation and the square-wave limit

The derivation in §10.3 needed $V_m \le V_c$. Push past it and the modulator does not break, but it stops being linear — and what it does instead is examinable.

Exam note — the comparison most likely to be asked

If a question says "compare bipolar and unipolar PWM", the marks are in three lines: same fundamental $mV_{DC}$; unipolar has three output levels and bipolar two; unipolar's first harmonic cluster is at $2m_f$, bipolar's at $m_f$. Add the voltage-jump point — $V_{DC}$ versus $2V_{DC}$ per edge, which matters for insulation stress and EMI — and the answer is complete.

10.7 What PWM costs

Watch out — the trade is losses, and the notes star it

The lecture notes mark the disadvantage with a star: higher switching losses. A six-step inverter switches each device twice per output cycle — 100 times a second at 50 Hz. A PWM inverter at $f_c = 10$ kHz switches each device 10,000 times a second, a factor of 100 more. With $E_{on}$ per event from §2.5, the loss scales directly with that.

So the design choice is: cheap filter and expensive switching (PWM), or cheap switching and expensive filter (six-step). High-power drives tend towards lower carrier frequencies for exactly this reason.

Checkpoint

Why does raising the carrier frequency not reduce the amplitude of the harmonics, only move them?

Because the amplitude of each sideband is set by the modulation index $m$, not by $m_f$. Raising $f_c$ relocates the clusters to higher frequency without shrinking them. What makes them harmless is not that they are smaller but that the load's own inductance has a higher impedance at those frequencies, so far less harmonic current flows — and current is what causes loss and torque ripple.

10.8 Three-phase SPWM — the voltage the motor actually sees

Everything so far has been one leg. A three-phase inverter is three of those legs sharing one DC link, and PWM Lecture 3 builds it in exactly that way: three reference sinusoids, $120°$ apart, each compared against the same triangular carrier. Each comparison drives one leg, so each leg behaves exactly like the half-bridge of §10.3 and has nothing to do with the other two.

The lecture defines the modulation index for this circuit as $m = A/A_m$ — reference amplitude over carrier amplitude — which is the same $V_m/V_c$ of §10.3 with different letters. Then it states the result that every drive calculation leans on.

From pole voltage to line voltage

Each leg's pole voltage, measured from the DC midpoint O, has the fundamental found in §10.3:

$$v_{aO,1} = m\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin\omega t,\qquad v_{bO,1} = m\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin(\omega t - 120°),\qquad v_{cO,1} = m\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin(\omega t - 240°)$$

The motor does not care about O. It sees two things: the line voltage between two terminals, and the phase voltage from a terminal to its own star point N. The line voltage is a difference of two pole voltages, and two sines of equal amplitude $120°$ apart differ by a sine $\sqrt3$ times bigger:

$$v_{ab,1} = v_{aO,1} - v_{bO,1} = \sqrt3\, m\frac{V_{DC}}{2}\sin(\omega t + 30°)$$

The phase voltage is the pole voltage minus the common-mode voltage $v_{NO}$ of §9.3. The common-mode part contains only triplen components — identical in all three phases — so it carries no fundamental. The fundamental of the phase voltage is therefore just the fundamental of the pole voltage. Collecting the three:

Key result — three-phase SPWM, linear region ($m \le 1$)
$$\boxed{V_{ph,1,pk} = \frac{mV_{DC}}{2}}\qquad V_{ph,1,rms} = \frac{mV_{DC}}{2\sqrt2} = 0.354\,mV_{DC}$$ $$\boxed{V_{LL,1,rms} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2}\,mV_{DC} = 0.612\,mV_{DC}}$$

The first line is PWM Lecture 3, slide 5, written out. The second is what a nameplate voltage is quoted in, so it is the form a drive question needs.

Why it happens — "clamped to $V_{DC}$ or 0"

The lecture says that past $m = 1$ the phase waveform "starts getting clamped to either $V_{DC}$ or 0". That phrasing measures the pole voltage from the negative rail N, where it swings between 0 and $V_{DC}$, rather than from the midpoint O, where it swings between $\pm V_{DC}/2$. The two descriptions differ only by a constant $V_{DC}/2$. Once the reference is taller than the carrier, there are whole carrier periods in which no crossing occurs, so the leg sits at one rail. This is the over-modulation picture of §10.6, seen from N instead of O.

Worked example 10.2 — how big must the DC link be?

A 400 V (line, RMS) motor is to receive full voltage from an SPWM inverter without leaving the linear region. Find the minimum DC link, and the modulation index if the link is actually 680 V.

Full voltage at the edge of the linear region means $m = 1$:

$$400 = 0.612\,V_{DC} \;\Longrightarrow\; V_{DC,min} = \frac{400}{0.6124} = 653\ \text{V}$$

With a 680 V link:

$$m = \frac{400}{0.6124 \times 680} = 0.961$$

A 400 V motor needs a link of at least 653 V under linear SPWM. That is more than the 540 V a 400 V diode bridge produces, so a drive on 400 V mains either over-modulates near rated speed or accepts a slightly lower top voltage. This is not an academic point: it is exactly why the drive questions of Chapter 11 give you a modulation index and ask for a firing angle — the rectifier's job is to supply whatever link voltage the inverter's $m$ demands.

10.9 When the carrier does not straddle zero

Every derivation in this chapter used a carrier symmetric about zero, $\pm V_c$, and a pole voltage measured from the midpoint O. Tutorial 10 Q1 changes both at once: the carrier runs from 0 to 5 V, and the load hangs between the leg's midpoint and the negative rail. Learning $D = \frac{V_m}{2V_c} + \frac12$ as a formula leaves you stuck. Knowing where it came from does not.

Go back to the mechanism. The top switch is on whenever the modulating signal is above the carrier. A triangular carrier rises and falls linearly between its minimum $c_{min}$ and its maximum $c_{max}$, so the fraction of each carrier period it spends below a constant level $v_m$ is simply how far up the range $v_m$ sits:

Key result — duty ratio from any triangular carrier
$$\boxed{D = \frac{v_m - c_{min}}{c_{max} - c_{min}}}\qquad \langle v_{AN}\rangle = D\,V_{DC},\qquad \langle v_{AO}\rangle = \left(D - \tfrac12\right)V_{DC}$$

With $c_{min} = -V_c$ and $c_{max} = +V_c$ this collapses to $D = \frac{v_m + V_c}{2V_c} = \frac{V_m}{2V_c} + \frac12$ — the lecture's result is the special case.

Watch out — read the circuit before you use a formula

The same gate signals give three different answers depending on where the load's other end is tied. Between the midpoint and N: $D\,V_{DC}$. Between the midpoint and a split-capacitor centre O: $(D-\tfrac12)V_{DC}$. Between two legs of a full bridge: the difference of two such terms. Tutorial 10 says "applying $V_{dc}$ across the load", which settles it. Full working is in §13.6.

Chapter 11 post-midterm · core

The Complete Drive Chain

Everything in this document exists to do one job: spin a motor at whatever speed you ask for. This chapter connects the rectifier, the inverter and the machine into one calculation — and it is the shape of a five-mark question.

11.1 Why an induction motor needs all of this

An induction motor's speed is set by the frequency of its supply:

$$N_s = \frac{120f}{P}$$

with $P$ the number of poles. Connect it straight to the 50 Hz mains and a 4-pole machine runs at very nearly 1500 rpm, always. To change the speed you must change the frequency, and the grid will not do that for you.

So: rectify the mains to DC, then invert that DC back to AC at whatever frequency you choose. That is the drive chain, and it is why rectifiers and inverters are taught in the same course.

11.2 Constant V/f — why voltage must track frequency

You cannot just lower the frequency and leave the voltage alone. Here is why, in one line of physics.

Why it happens — the flux would saturate the core

The stator's back-EMF is $E \approx 4.44\,N\phi f$, so for a fixed applied voltage $V \approx E$:

$$\phi \;\propto\; \frac{V}{f}$$

Halve the frequency at constant voltage and you double the flux. The core saturates, the magnetising current explodes, and the machine overheats without producing useful torque.

Keep $V/f$ constant and $\phi$ stays at its design value. Since torque $T = k\,\phi\,I_r$, constant flux also means the machine keeps its full torque-producing capability at every speed. The notes carry this through to $T_{max} \propto (V/f)^2$ — holding $V/f$ fixed holds the breakdown torque fixed too.

Key result — the V/f scaling rule
$$\frac{V_1}{f_1} = \frac{V_{rated}}{f_{rated}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad V_{new} = \frac{N_{s,new}}{N_{s,rated}}\,V_{rated}$$

The right-hand form is the practical one: scale the voltage by the ratio of synchronous speeds, because synchronous speed is proportional to frequency.

11.3 Slip, and the fact that makes the problem solvable

An induction motor never runs at synchronous speed — it must slip to induce rotor current. Define the slip speed as the difference:

$$\omega_{sl} = N_s - N_r$$
Key result — slip speed is proportional to torque
$$\text{At constant flux:}\qquad \omega_{sl} \;\propto\; T$$

The notes derive this at length (ending in $T \propto a\,s$, and $a\,s \propto \omega_{sl}$), but the usable form is the proportionality. Half the torque, half the slip speed.

Watch out — slip speed scales with torque, not with speed

This is the single most slippery step in the whole calculation. At 50% torque the slip speed halves regardless of what speed the motor is running at. It does not scale with the frequency, the voltage, or the rotor speed. Get this wrong and every number downstream is wrong.

11.4 The whole chain, worked

Procedure — motor operating point back to firing angle
  1. Rated synchronous speed: $N_s = 120f/P$.
  2. Rated slip speed: $N_s - N_{rated}$.
  3. Slip speed at the required torque: scale it by the torque fraction.
  4. Required synchronous speed: the target rotor speed plus that slip speed.
  5. Required voltage from the V/f rule: scale $V_{rated}$ by the ratio of synchronous speeds. This gives a line-to-line RMS value.
  6. Convert to peak phase: divide by $\sqrt3$, multiply by $\sqrt2$.
  7. Back through the inverter: $V_{ph,pk} = m\,V_{DC}/2$, so $V_{DC} = 2V_{ph,pk}/m$.
  8. Back through the rectifier: $V_{DC} = \frac{3}{\pi}V_{LL,pk}\cos\alpha$, so solve for $\alpha$.
Worked example 11.1 — Quiz Q7, 5 marks, worked by the examiner

A three-phase, 400 V, 50 Hz, 1420 rpm, 4-pole, star-connected squirrel-cage induction motor is fed from a PWM VSI operating at constant flux. The VSI is fed from a three-phase fully controlled converter on a 440 V, 50 Hz mains. The modulation index is 0.8. Find the firing angle of the converter for a speed of 560 rpm at 50% of torque.

1. Rated synchronous speed.

$$N_s = \frac{120 \times 50}{4} = 1500\ \text{rpm}$$

2. Rated slip speed. $\;1500 - 1420 = 80$ rpm.

3. Slip speed at 50% torque. Slip speed tracks torque, so $\;80/2 = 40$ rpm.

4. Synchronous speed needed. The rotor must turn at 560 rpm and slip by 40, so the field must rotate at

$$N_{s,new} = 560 + 40 = 600\ \text{rpm}$$

5. Voltage from constant V/f.

$$V_{LL} = \frac{600}{1500}\times 400 = 160\ \text{V (RMS, line-to-line)}$$

6. Peak phase voltage.

$$V_{ph,pk} = \frac{160}{\sqrt3}\times\sqrt2 = 92.38 \times 1.4142 = 130.64\ \text{V}$$

7. Back through the inverter. With $m = 0.8$:

$$m\frac{V_{DC}}{2} = 130.64 \;\Longrightarrow\; V_{DC} = \frac{2 \times 130.64}{0.8} = 326.6\ \text{V}$$

8. Back through the rectifier. The front end is a three-phase full converter on 440 V:

$$V_{DC} = \frac{3\sqrt2\,V_{LL}}{\pi}\cos\alpha \;\Longrightarrow\; \cos\alpha = \frac{326.6 \times \pi}{3\sqrt2 \times 440} = \frac{1026.0}{1866.8} = 0.5496$$ $$\boxed{\alpha = 56.7°}$$

Notice how little of this is power electronics and how much is bookkeeping — the marks are in steps 3, 4 and 6, every one of which is a place to slip. Step 3: slip speed scales with torque, not speed. Step 4: the synchronous speed is the rotor speed plus slip, not minus. Step 5: the V/f ratio uses $600/1500$, the synchronous speeds — using $560/1420$ is the classic error and gives 157.7 V instead of 160 V. Step 6: the inverter relation needs peak phase, so both conversions must happen.

And the result is worth reading physically: to run this motor at 37% of its rated speed you fire the front-end converter at 56.7°, pulling the DC link down to 327 V from the 594 V it could supply. The drive is throttling the whole chain from the mains end.

Constant V/f cannot continue forever. Once the frequency reaches its rated value the voltage has reached rated too, and the supply has nothing more to give.

Checkpoint

A drive runs a motor at twice its base speed with the voltage held at rated. What has happened to the available torque, and why?

It has halved. With $V$ fixed and $f$ doubled, $\phi \propto V/f$ halves, and $T = k\phi I_r$ halves with it. The power stays constant, which is why this is the constant-power region — the same reason a car gearbox trades torque for speed.

11.6 When is an operating point stable?

A motor and its load meet where their torque–speed curves cross. But not every crossing is a place the system will actually sit — nudge it, and it may run away.

Start from the equation of motion, then perturb every quantity slightly and subtract the steady-state equation:

$$T = T_L + J\frac{d\omega_m}{dt} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta T = \Delta T_L + J\frac{d(\Delta\omega_m)}{dt}$$

Over a small perturbation both curves are straight, so $\Delta T = \frac{dT}{d\omega_m}\Delta\omega_m$ and $\Delta T_L = \frac{dT_L}{d\omega_m}\Delta\omega_m$. Substituting gives a first-order equation whose solution is

$$\Delta\omega_m = C\exp\left(-\frac{1}{J}\left[\frac{dT_L}{d\omega_m} - \frac{dT}{d\omega_m}\right]t\right)$$
Key result — the stability criterion
$$\boxed{\;\frac{dT_L}{d\omega_m} > \frac{dT}{d\omega_m}\;}$$

The perturbation decays — and the point is stable — only if that exponent is negative, which requires the load's slope to exceed the motor's.

Why it happens — relative slopes, not either curve alone

The lecture notes state this conclusion in prose, which is their signal that it is to be understood rather than memorised: "Stability of operating point does not depend on the characteristics speed-torque of motor load system but it depends on the relative nature of the two points of concern."

Watch it work both ways on the same motor:

  • Constant-torque load. A horizontal $T_L$ line cuts the induction-motor curve twice. The intersection past the breakdown torque, on the falling side, is stable; the one on the rising side is unstable.
  • Fan load. $T_L \propto \omega^2$ rises steeply and cuts the rising side of that same motor curve — and that point is stable, because the fan's slope beats the motor's.

Same motor, same side of the curve, opposite verdict. That is the whole content of the criterion.

11.7 Slip-speed control — the fix for open-loop V/f

Exam note — the lecturer starred this one

There are only three starred items in the entire post-midterm note stream: the PWM switching-loss penalty (§10.5), "huge filter needed" for the square-wave inverter (§8.3), and "slip frequency control" — written immediately after the list of what is wrong with open-loop V/f. He is flagging the progression V/f limitations → slip-speed control as something to be able to walk through.

Constant V/f as described so far is open loop, and the notes list its three weaknesses bluntly: no control on motor current, dynamics are not controlled, open loop control. If the load steps, nothing limits the current the machine draws.

Slip-speed control fixes that by commanding the slip speed directly rather than the frequency. Define the frequency ratio $a = f/f_{rated}$. Below base speed ($a<1$) the voltage scales with it, $V = a\,V_{rated}$, holding the flux constant. The rotor current is then

$$I_r' = \frac{a\,V_{rated}}{\sqrt{(R_r'/s)^2 + a^2(X_s+X_r')^2}} \;\xrightarrow[\;R_r'/s \,\gg\, X\;]{s \,\ll\, 1}\; \frac{V_{rated}}{R_r'}\,a\,s$$

and the torque becomes $T = \dfrac{3}{\omega_{ms}}\cdot\dfrac{V_{rated}^2}{R_r'}\cdot a\,s$.

Now the substitution that makes the method work. Since $s = (a\omega_{sm} - \omega_m)/(a\omega_{sm})$, the product $a\,s = (a\omega_{sm} - \omega_m)/\omega_{sm}$, which is proportional to the slip speed $\omega_{sl}$. Therefore:

Key result — torque and current both track slip speed
$$T \propto \omega_{sl} \qquad\qquad I_s \propto \omega_{sl}$$

In the lecturer's own words: "for any given slip speed, the torque & current is const. irrespective of frequency of supply."

Commanding $\omega_{sl}$ sets the torque and bounds the current at the same time, at every speed — exactly what open-loop V/f could not do. Put a limiter on the slip-speed command and current protection is built into the control law rather than bolted on afterwards.

Above base speed ($a>1$) the voltage is capped at $V_{rated}$ and the flux falls. The same algebra gives $I_s \approx \frac{V_{rated}}{R_r'}\cdot\frac{\omega_{sl}}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{\omega_{sm}}$, so holding current constant means holding $\omega_{sl}/a$ constant — and then $T \propto 1/a$.

The closed loop, as the lecture notes draw it
  1. $\omega_{ref}$ minus the measured $\omega_m$ gives the speed error.
  2. A speed controller (PI or higher order) acts on that error.
  3. Its output passes a limiter — and this is the whole point, because the limited quantity is $\omega_{sl}^*$, which bounds both torque and current.
  4. Add $\omega_m$ back to get the commanded synchronous speed.
  5. Scale by $\dfrac{P/2}{2\pi}$ to get the commanded frequency $f^*$.
  6. $f^*$ drives the V/f profile block, which outputs $V^*$.
  7. $f^*$ and $V^*$ build the three modulating signals $V_m\sin\omega t$, $V_m\sin(\omega t-120°)$, $V_m\sin(\omega t-240°)$.
  8. A comparator against the triangular carrier $V_c$ produces gate pulses T1–T6 ($m = V_m/V_c$, from §10.3).
Key result — motor efficiency
$$\eta_{motor} = \frac{P_m}{P_g} = 1 - s$$

Air-gap power is $P_g = 3I_r'^2R_r'/s$; mechanical output is that minus the rotor copper loss $3I_r'^2R_r'$, leaving $3I_r'^2R_r'(1-s)/s$. The ratio is exactly $1-s$.

This is why induction motors run at small slip — and why slip-speed control, which keeps slip small and known, is efficient as well as safe.

11.8 Variable terminal voltage — and why it is the weak method

The crudest speed control is to vary the stator voltage alone, leaving the frequency at 50 Hz. From the torque expression with everything else fixed:

$$T \propto V^2, \qquad T_{max} \propto V^2, \qquad I_r' \propto V$$
Watch out — why this method barely works

Reducing $V$ shrinks the breakdown torque quadratically while the synchronous speed does not move at all. Whatever speed change you get comes entirely from sliding down to a higher slip — so the range is small, and by $\eta = 1-s$ every bit of it is paid for in rotor heating.

If asked to compare the methods, that is the comparison in one line: V/f shifts the synchronous speed; voltage control only increases the slip.

Checkpoint

A motor runs at 4% slip. Ignoring every loss except rotor copper loss, what is the best efficiency it can have?

$\eta = 1-s = 0.96$. The 4% that slips away is dissipated in the rotor as $I^2R$ and cannot be recovered — which is exactly why §11.8's method is poor: it buys speed by increasing the very quantity that sets the loss.

11.9 What a drive is for — constant torque, then constant power

PWM Lecture 4 steps back from the arithmetic and asks what a drive is supposed to deliver. The answer is a shape on a torque–speed plot that neither an induction motor nor a synchronous motor can produce by itself.

A vehicle, a hoist or a conveyor needs full torque at low speed to break static friction and accelerate. Once moving, it needs speed more than torque. So the ideal machine holds rated torque from standstill up to a base speed $\omega_b$, then holds rated power beyond it, trading torque for speed along $T = P_{max}/\omega$.

Why can't the motor do this on its own? Look back at the natural torque–speed curve of §11.6. An induction motor on a fixed 50 Hz supply has one stable stretch — the steep part near synchronous speed — and no flat torque region at all. Its speed is pinned near $N_s$, and $N_s$ is pinned by the supply frequency. The drive's job is to move that stable stretch to wherever the load needs it, by moving $f$ and $V$ together. The envelope in Figure 11.3 is traced out by the tips of a whole family of shifted curves, one per frequency.

The permanent-magnet synchronous motor (PMSM)

The lecture adds a second machine. A synchronous motor's rotor turns at exactly synchronous speed — zero slip — at every torque up to pull-out. Its torque–speed curve is a vertical line at $N_s = 120f/P$. In a PMSM the rotor field comes from magnets on or inside the rotor, so no separate excitation supply is needed.

Neither an IM nor a PMSM, on a fixed-frequency supply, has a stable constant-torque region or a stable constant-power region. A drive provides both: constant V/f below base speed gives constant flux and therefore constant torque capability; constant V with rising $f$ above base speed weakens the field and gives constant power. A PMSM resists the second because its flux comes from magnets.

Checkpoint

A drive runs a 4-pole PMSM from 50 Hz rated. The load doubles its torque demand. What happens to the speed? What would an induction motor have done?

The PMSM's speed does not change: it stays at $120(50)/4 = 1500$ rpm, because a synchronous machine has no slip — only its load angle increases. An induction motor at constant flux would slow down: by §11.3 its slip speed doubles, so it drops further below 1500 rpm.

12.1 Q1 — Three-phase semiconverter battery charger

A 3-phase semiconverter charges a battery from a 3-phase 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Battery EMF 200 V, internal resistance 0.5 Ω, a large inductor keeps the charging current constant at 20 A. Find (a) the firing angle, (b) the supply power factor.

Method
  1. DC side first: $V_{dc} = E + I_o R_{int}$.
  2. Semiconverter law: $V_{dc} = \frac{3V_{LL,pk}}{2\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$. Solve for $\alpha$.
  3. Check whether $\alpha \gtrless 60°$ — that decides the source current waveform, because freewheeling begins above $60°$.
  4. $I_s = I_o\sqrt{2/3}$ if $\alpha \le 60°$; $I_s = I_o\sqrt{(\pi-\alpha)/\pi}$ if $\alpha > 60°$.
  5. $\text{PF} = \dfrac{P_{dc}}{\sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_s}$ with $P_{dc} = V_{dc}I_o$.
$$V_{dc} = 200 + 20(0.5) = 210\ \text{V} \qquad V_{LL,pk} = 230\sqrt2 = 325.27\ \text{V}$$ $$210 = \frac{3(325.27)}{2\pi}(1+\cos\alpha) = 155.30(1+\cos\alpha) \;\Rightarrow\; \cos\alpha = 0.3522 \;\Rightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\alpha = 69.4°}$$

$\alpha > 60°$, so the leg freewheels and the source current is zero for part of each half-cycle:

$$I_s = 20\sqrt{\frac{180-69.38}{180}} = 15.68\ \text{A} \qquad P = 210 \times 20 = 4200\ \text{W}$$ $$\text{PF} = \frac{4200}{\sqrt3(230)(15.68)} = \boldsymbol{0.672\ \text{lagging}}$$
Watch out — two traps in one question

First, the $60°$ check is not optional. Using $I_s = I_o\sqrt{2/3} = 16.33$ A here would give PF = 0.645 — wrong, and wrong in a way that looks plausible.

Second, the sheet writes plain "230 V" without saying whether it is line-to-line or per-phase, and it is inconsistent about this from question to question (Q4 says "neutral to any line", Q5 says "per phase", Q8 says $V_{LL}$). Taken as per-phase instead, $V_{LL} = 398.4$ V and the answers become $\alpha = 102.7°$, PF = 0.464. Write down which you assumed. A stated assumption cannot be marked wrong; a silent one can.

12.3 Q3 — Three-phase bridge in inverting mode

415 V supply, 0.25 Ω reactance per phase, inverting at $\alpha = 145°$, current 80 A, resistance 0.04 Ω per phase, thyristor drop 1.5 V. Find the generator voltage $E$.

$$V_{do} = \frac{3\sqrt2(415)}{\pi} = 560.45\ \text{V}$$ $$V_{dc} = V_{do}\cos 145° - \frac{3X_s}{\pi}I_o = -459.09 - \frac{3(0.25)}{\pi}(80) = -459.09 - 19.10 = -478.19\ \text{V}$$

The machine must drive against that back-voltage plus every drop in the DC loop. Two phases carry the DC current at any instant, so the AC-side resistance appears doubled, and two thyristors conduct in series:

$$E = 478.19 + 2(0.04)(80) + 2(1.5) = 478.19 + 6.4 + 3 = \boldsymbol{487.6\ \text{V}}$$
Watch out — the sign that catches everyone

Keep the single equation $V_{dc} = V_{do}\cos\alpha - \frac{3X_s}{\pi}I_o$ for all $\alpha$, and let the arithmetic produce the negative value by itself. At $\alpha > 90°$ the commutation term makes $|V_{dc}|$ larger, so the generator EMF must be larger too. Flipping that sign — "it's inverting, so the drop must help" — is the classic error here.

And note the two factors of 2: $R_{eq} = 2R_{phase}$ and device drop $= 2V_T$, both because two devices and two phases are in the conducting loop.

12.4 Q4 — Three-phase half-wave battery charger (discontinuous)

Input neutral-to-line 230 V rms, $\alpha = 30°$, load is 5 Ω in series with a 150 V battery. Find the battery current.

No inductor and a back-EMF, so current only flows while the supply exceeds $E$. This is a discontinuous-conduction problem and the average-voltage formula does not apply.

Method
  1. $\theta_1 = 30° + \alpha$ (the $30°$ is the natural commutation point).
  2. Check $V_{s,pk}\sin\theta_1 > E$, else conduction starts later.
  3. $\theta_2 = 180° - \sin^{-1}(E/V_{s,pk})$, unless the next device fires first.
  4. $I_{avg} = \dfrac{3}{2\pi R}\Big[V_{s,pk}(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2) - E(\theta_2-\theta_1)\Big]$, with the angles in radians.
$$V_{s,pk} = 230\sqrt2 = 325.27\ \text{V},\qquad \theta_1 = 60°$$ $$325.27\sin 60° = 281.7\ \text{V} > 150\ \text{V} \;\checkmark$$ $$\sin^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{150}{325.27}\right) = 27.46° \;\Rightarrow\; \theta_2 = 152.54°$$ $$I_{avg} = \frac{3}{2\pi(5)}\Big[325.27(0.5 + 0.8871) - 150(2.6622-1.0472)\Big] = \frac{3(451.19 - 242.25)}{31.42} = \boldsymbol{19.96\ \text{A}}$$

12.9 Q9 — Tapped-inductor converter

$V_{in} = 50$ V, $S_1$ on for 70 µs and $S_2$ for 30 µs complementarily, $n_1 = 4$, $n_2 = 3$. Find $V_o$.

Why it happens — balance volts per turn, not volt-seconds

A different number of turns is energised in each sub-interval, so plain volt-second balance does not apply. What must return to its starting value is the flux, and flux is driven by volts per turn:

  • $S_1$ on: current flows through both windings in series, so $\dfrac{V_{in}-V_o}{n_1+n_2}$ volts per turn.
  • $S_2$ on: current flows through $n_2$ only, so $\dfrac{-V_o}{n_2}$.
$$\frac{(V_{in}-V_o)D}{n_1+n_2} = \frac{V_o(1-D)}{n_2}$$
$$V_o = \frac{V_{in}D\,n_2}{D\,n_2 + (1-D)(n_1+n_2)} = \frac{50(0.7)(3)}{(0.7)(3)+(0.3)(7)} = \frac{105}{2.1+2.1} = \boldsymbol{25\ \text{V}}$$

The two denominator terms happen to be equal here, which is why the answer lands exactly on $V_{in}/2$ — a coincidence of the numbers, not a general result.

13.1 Tut 8&9 Q1 — Is this buck in CCM? And what if the load lightens?

A buck converter has $V_o = 5$ V, $V_{in} = 10$ V, $f_{sw} = 20$ kHz, $L = 500\ \mu$H, $C = 400\ \mu$F, $R_L = 5\ \Omega$. Identify CCM or DCM and find $\Delta V_o$. (a) Find $R_L$ at the CCM/DCM boundary. (b) With $R_L$ twice that value, find $\Delta V_o$.

Method — the CCM/DCM test
  1. Assume CCM and get $D$ from the CCM gain ($D = V_o/V_{in}$ for a buck).
  2. Compute $K = \dfrac{2L}{R\,T_s}$.
  3. Compare with $K_{crit}$ for that topology at that $D$. $K > K_{crit}$ → CCM, the assumption holds. $K < K_{crit}$ → DCM, and the CCM gain was wrong: redo with the DCM gain.
$$D = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5,\qquad T_s = 50\ \mu\text{s},\qquad K = \frac{2(500\times10^{-6})}{5(50\times10^{-6})} = 4,\qquad K_{crit} = 1 - D = 0.5$$

$K = 4 \gg 0.5$, so the converter is firmly in CCM. Cross-check with currents: $\Delta i_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_o)DT_s}{L} = \frac{5(0.5)(50\ \mu)}{500\ \mu} = 0.25$ A, and the boundary would need $I_o = \Delta i_L/2 = 0.125$ A, against an actual $I_o = 1$ A. Same verdict.

For the buck, the capacitor sees only the triangular part of $i_L$ (§7.2):

$$\Delta V_o = \frac{\Delta i_L}{8Cf_{sw}} = \frac{0.25}{8(400\times10^{-6})(20\,000)} = \boldsymbol{3.91\ \text{mV}}\quad(0.078\%\ \text{of } V_o)$$

(a) Boundary load. Set $K = K_{crit}$ and solve for $R$:

$$\frac{2L}{R\,T_s} = 1 - D \;\Longrightarrow\; R_{B} = \frac{2Lf_{sw}}{1-D} = \frac{2(500\ \mu)(20\,000)}{0.5} = \boldsymbol{40\ \Omega}$$

Same thing from currents: at the boundary $I_o = 0.125$ A, so $R = 5/0.125 = 40\ \Omega$.

(b) $R_L = 80\ \Omega$. Now $K = 4 \times 5/80 = 0.25 < 0.5$ — the converter is in DCM, and the inductor current sits at zero for part of each period. Two things change, and the question only names one.

The output voltage changes. If the duty ratio is held at 0.5 (open loop), the DCM gain of §7.4 applies:

$$\frac{V_o}{V_{in}} = \frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}} = \frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4}} = 0.618 \;\Longrightarrow\; V_o = 6.18\ \text{V}$$

The ripple formula changes. $\Delta i_L/8Cf$ assumed the capacitor current was a symmetric triangle about zero. In DCM it is not, so go back to the definition: the ripple is the charge the capacitor gains while $i_L > I_o$, divided by $C$.

$$I_{pk} = \frac{(V_{in}-V_o)DT_s}{L} = \frac{3.82(0.5)(50\ \mu)}{500\ \mu} = 0.191\ \text{A},\qquad I_o = \frac{6.18}{80} = 0.0773\ \text{A}$$ $$D_2 = \frac{D(V_{in}-V_o)}{V_o} = \frac{0.5(3.82)}{6.18} = 0.309$$ $$\Delta Q = \tfrac12\,\frac{(0.191-0.0773)^2}{0.191}\,(0.809)(50\ \mu) = 1.37\ \mu\text{C} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta V_o = \frac{1.37\ \mu}{400\ \mu} = \boldsymbol{3.42\ \text{mV}}$$
Worked example 13.1 — the other reading of part (b)

If the converter is regulated — $V_o$ held at 5 V by adjusting $D$ — then solve the DCM gain for $D$ instead:

$$\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}} = 0.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1 + \frac{4K}{D^2} = 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; D = \sqrt{K/2} = 0.354$$

Then $I_{pk} = 5(0.354)(50\ \mu)/500\ \mu = 0.177$ A, $I_o = 0.0625$ A, $D_2 = 0.354$, and the same triangle gives $\Delta V_o = 3.26$ mV.

Both readings give a ripple smaller than the CCM 3.91 mV, which surprises people. Lighter load means smaller current pulses, so less charge goes into the capacitor per cycle. DCM is bad for regulation (the gain now depends on load) and for peak current stress at heavy load — not, at light load, for ripple. Write down which reading you used; the sheet does not say.

13.2 Tut 8&9 Q2 — Prove the buck-boost results

Prove that for the buck-boost converter $K_{crit} = (1-D)^2$, and that in DCM $\dfrac{V_o}{V_{in}} = -\dfrac{D}{\sqrt K}$ where $K = \dfrac{2L}{R_LT_s}$.

This is a four-mark proof, and the marks are in the setup, not the algebra. Both halves use the same two facts about the buck-boost from §6.4: the inductor carries input plus output current, and it is charged from $V_{in}$ while the switch is on and discharged into $V_o$ while the diode is on.

Part 1 — the boundary

  1. In CCM the inductor's average current is $I_L = I_{in} + I_o$. Power balance gives $I_{in} = I_o D/(1-D)$, so $I_L = \dfrac{I_o}{1-D} = \dfrac{|V_o|}{R(1-D)}$.
  2. The ripple, from the on-interval: $\Delta i_L = \dfrac{V_{in}DT_s}{L}$.
  3. At the boundary the current just touches zero: $I_L = \Delta i_L/2$.
  4. Substitute $|V_o| = V_{in}D/(1-D)$: $$\frac{V_{in}D}{R(1-D)^2} = \frac{V_{in}DT_s}{2L} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{2L}{RT_s} = (1-D)^2$$

The left side is $K$, so at the boundary $\boxed{K_{crit} = (1-D)^2}$. CCM needs $K > K_{crit}$.

Part 2 — the DCM gain

In DCM the switch is on for $DT_s$, the diode for $D_2T_s$, and the inductor is idle for the rest. $D_2$ is a new unknown, so we need two equations.

  1. Volt-second balance (magnitudes): $V_{in}DT_s = |V_o|D_2T_s \;\Rightarrow\; D_2 = \dfrac{V_{in}D}{|V_o|}$.
  2. Charge balance on the capacitor. In a buck-boost all of the load's charge comes through the diode, so the diode's average current equals $I_o$. The diode carries the falling half of a triangle of height $I_{pk} = V_{in}DT_s/L$ for $D_2T_s$: $$\tfrac12 I_{pk}D_2 = \frac{|V_o|}{R} \;\Longrightarrow\; D_2 = \frac{2L\,|V_o|}{RT_s\,V_{in}D} = \frac{K|V_o|}{V_{in}D}$$
  3. Equate the two expressions for $D_2$: $$\frac{V_{in}D}{|V_o|} = \frac{K|V_o|}{V_{in}D} \;\Longrightarrow\; \left(\frac{|V_o|}{V_{in}}\right)^2 = \frac{D^2}{K}$$
  4. The buck-boost inverts, so restore the sign: $\boxed{\dfrac{V_o}{V_{in}} = -\dfrac{D}{\sqrt K}}$.
Key result — the three DCM laws, side by side
$K_{crit}$DCM gain $V_o/V_{in}$Charge-balance device
Buck$1-D$$\dfrac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}}$inductor ($i_L$ average $= I_o$)
Boost$D(1-D)^2$$\dfrac{1+\sqrt{1+4D^2/K}}{2}$diode
Buck-boost$(1-D)^2$$-\dfrac{D}{\sqrt K}$diode

The boost row is derived the same way: volt-seconds give $D_2 = V_{in}D/(V_o - V_{in})$, diode charge gives $D_2 = KV_o/(V_{in}D)$, and equating gives $M^2 - M - D^2/K = 0$ with $M = V_o/V_{in}$. The positive root is the entry above. It is worked as a model answer in §14.3.

Watch out — which current does charge balance use?

In the buck the inductor feeds the output continuously, so its average equals $I_o$. In the boost and the buck-boost the output is fed only through the diode, so it is the diode's average that equals $I_o$. Using the inductor average for a buck-boost gives $(\tfrac12 I_{pk})(D+D_2) = I_o$, which is wrong by a term in $D$, and the $\sqrt K$ never appears.

13.4 Tut 8&9 Q4 — An unfamiliar Kcrit

For a DC–DC converter $K_{crit} = 1 - 3D + 2D^2$. Find $L$ for the converter to be in DCM with a 20% safety margin. Load 20 Ω, 20 kHz.

The point of the question is that you do not need to know the converter. The test $K \lessgtr K_{crit}$ is the same for every topology; only the expression for $K_{crit}$ changes. DCM means $K < K_{crit}$, which is an upper limit on $L$:

$$\frac{2L}{RT_s} < K_{crit} \;\Longrightarrow\; L < L_{crit} = \frac{K_{crit}\,R}{2f_{sw}} = K_{crit}\times\frac{20}{2(20\,000)} = K_{crit}\times 0.5\ \text{mH}$$

A 20% margin on the DCM side means designing 20% below the limit: $L = 0.8\,L_{crit} = K_{crit} \times 0.4$ mH.

The sheet gives no duty ratio, so look at the expression. It factorises: $K_{crit} = (1-D)(1-2D)$. It is largest, 1, at $D \to 0$, falls to zero at $D = 0.5$, and is negative between 0.5 and 1. A negative $K_{crit}$ means $K > K_{crit}$ always: this converter cannot enter DCM for $D \ge 0.5$.

    answer

    answer

    answer
    (a), (b) and (d) — three statements of one condition. (d) is the physical reading: if speeding up makes the load win, the system decelerates back. §11.6.

B1 — Single-phase semi-controlled converter, and a failed thyristor [5+5]

A 1-φ semi-controlled bridge (T1, T2 common-cathode; D1, D2 common-anode) on 230 V, 50 Hz feeds $R = 4\ \Omega$, $E = 50$ V and a large inductor, at $\alpha = 40°$. (a) Find $I_o$, the average and RMS current of T1 and the RMS supply current; sketch $v_o$, $i_{T1}$, $i_{D1}$, $i_s$. (b) T2 fails open-circuit. Find the new $I_o$ and redraw.

model answer

(a) $V_{s,pk} = 230\sqrt2 = 325.27$ V.

$$V_o = \frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha) = 103.54(1.766) = 182.85\ \text{V},\qquad I_o = \frac{182.85-50}{4} = 33.21\ \text{A}$$

Conduction, per cycle: T1 fires at $\alpha$ and conducts with D2 until $\pi$, when $v_s$ reverses. D1 then takes over from D2 and the load freewheels through T1–D1 (same leg, $v_o = 0$) until T2 fires at $\pi + \alpha$. So T1 conducts $180°$, from $\alpha$ to $\pi+\alpha$, and each diode conducts $180°$ starting at a zero crossing.

$$I_{T1,avg} = \frac{I_o}{2} = 16.61\ \text{A},\quad I_{T1,rms} = \frac{I_o}{\sqrt2} = 23.48\ \text{A},\quad I_{s,rms} = I_o\sqrt{\frac{\pi-\alpha}{\pi}} = 33.21\sqrt{\tfrac{140}{180}} = 29.29\ \text{A}$$

Sketch: $v_o$ follows $|v_s|$ from $\alpha$ to $\pi$, zero from $\pi$ to $\pi+\alpha$; $i_{T1}$ a block of $I_o$ from $40°$ to $220°$; $i_{D1}$ a block from $180°$ to $360°$; $i_s$ is $+I_o$ on $(40°, 180°)$, zero on $(180°, 220°)$, $-I_o$ on $(220°, 360°)$.

(b) This is where students write $\frac{V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$ and lose the marks. Follow T1. With a large inductor the current never stops, so after $\pi$ it freewheels through T1–D1 — and nothing ever turns T1 off, because the only thing that could commutate it was T2. At the next zero crossing $v_s$ goes positive, D2 takes over from D1, and T1–D2 connect the supply to the load at $0°$, not at $\alpha$. The firing angle has lost control:

$$V_o = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}V_{s,pk}\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi} = 103.54\ \text{V},\qquad I_o = \frac{103.54-50}{4} = \boldsymbol{13.38\ \text{A}}$$

Sketch: $v_o$ is the positive half-sine from $0$ to $\pi$ and zero for the whole negative half; T1 conducts continuously; D2 conducts $(0,\pi)$, D1 $(\pi,2\pi)$; $i_s$ is $+I_o$ for the positive half and zero for the negative half — it now has a DC component.

Checked by time-domain simulation: 103.54 V and 13.38 A. The result depends on continuous current. With a finite inductor, as in the real ETE Q3 ($L = 10$ mH), the freewheeling current may die before $2\pi$. Then T1 does turn off and is re-fired at $\alpha$, and the answer moves towards $\frac{V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$. State which case you are in, and say why.

B2 — The drive chain [10]

A 3-φ, 415 V, 50 Hz, 1440 rpm, 4-pole star motor is fed at constant flux by an SPWM VSI with $m = 0.9$, which is fed by a 3-φ fully controlled rectifier on 415 V mains. Find the firing angle for 700 rpm at 60% of full-load torque.

model answer
$$N_s = 1500,\quad \omega_{sl,rated} = 60\ \text{rpm},\quad \omega_{sl} = 0.6 \times 60 = 36\ \text{rpm}$$ $$N_{s,new} = 700 + 36 = 736\ \text{rpm}\;(f = 24.53\ \text{Hz}),\qquad V_{LL} = 415 \times \frac{736}{1500} = 203.6\ \text{V}$$ $$V_{ph,pk} = \frac{203.6\sqrt2}{\sqrt3} = 166.26\ \text{V},\qquad V_{DC} = \frac{2(166.26)}{0.9} = 369.5\ \text{V}$$ $$\cos\alpha = \frac{369.5\,\pi}{3\sqrt2(415)} = 0.6592 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\alpha = 48.8°}$$

The two traps are in the second line: slip speed scales with torque (0.6), and the V/f ratio uses the synchronous speeds 736/1500, not 700/1440.

B3 — Buck design with a minimum frequency [2+2+2+1+3]

Buck: $V_{in} = 120$ V, $V_o = 48$ V, $f_{sw} = 25$ kHz, $P_o = 240$ W, $\Delta V_o \le 2\%$, $\Delta i_L \le 10\%$. Find $L$, $C$, switch RMS current, average diode current, and the minimum $f_{sw}$ that keeps the converter in CCM with this $L$.

model answer
$$D = 0.4,\quad I_o = 5\ \text{A},\quad R = 9.6\ \Omega,\quad \Delta i_L = 0.5\ \text{A},\quad \Delta V_o = 0.96\ \text{V}$$ $$L = \frac{V_o(1-D)}{\Delta i_Lf_{sw}} = \frac{48(0.6)}{0.5(25\,000)} = \boldsymbol{2.30\ \text{mH}},\qquad C = \frac{\Delta i_L}{8\Delta V_of_{sw}} = \frac{0.5}{8(0.96)(25\,000)} = \boldsymbol{2.60\ \mu\text{F}}$$ $$I_{S,rms} = I_o\sqrt D = \boldsymbol{3.16\ \text{A}},\qquad I_{D,avg} = I_o(1-D) = \boldsymbol{3.0\ \text{A}}$$

CCM needs $K > K_{crit}$: $\frac{2Lf_{sw}}{R} > 1-D$, so

$$f_{sw,min} = \frac{(1-D)R}{2L} = \frac{0.6(9.6)}{2(2.304\times10^{-3})} = \boldsymbol{1.25\ \text{kHz}}$$

Cross-check: lowering $f$ by a factor $k$ raises $\Delta i_L$ by $k$, and the boundary is $\Delta i_L = 2I_o = 10$ A — twenty times the design ripple. $25/20 = 1.25$ kHz. The design sits far inside CCM, which is what a 10% ripple spec always gives you.

B4 — The capacitive-filter rectifier: prove, then size [5+5]

(a) For a single-phase diode bridge with a capacitive filter, show that $C = \dfrac{\pi-\alpha}{\pi}\cdot\dfrac{P_o}{2V_o\,\Delta V_o\,f_s}$, where $\alpha$ is the diode conduction angle per half-cycle. (b) 230 V, 50 Hz, 1 kW, ripple $\le 4\%$ of peak: find $C$. This fills the gap the 2025 end-term exposed — the same question carried 10 marks there.

model answer

(a) Over each half-cycle the diodes conduct for $\alpha$, ending approximately at the peak. For the rest, $\pi - \alpha$ radians or $t_d = \frac{\pi-\alpha}{\omega} = \frac{\pi-\alpha}{2\pi f_s}$ seconds, the capacitor supplies the load current $I_o = P_o/V_o$, which is nearly constant because the ripple is small. The charge it gives up is the charge its voltage records:

$$C\,\Delta V_o = I_o t_d = \frac{P_o}{V_o}\cdot\frac{\pi-\alpha}{2\pi f_s} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{C = \frac{\pi-\alpha}{\pi}\cdot\frac{P_o}{2V_o\,\Delta V_o\,f_s}}$$

Here $f_s$ is the supply frequency. The factor 2 is there because a bridge recharges the capacitor twice per cycle.

(b) $V_{pk} = 325.27$ V, $\Delta V_o = 0.04 \times 325.27 = 13.01$ V. Conduction begins where the sine climbs back to the trough, $\sin\theta = 0.96 \Rightarrow \theta = 73.74°$, so $\alpha = 90° - 73.74° = 16.26°$. Take $V_o$ as the mean output, $325.27 - 13.01/2 = 318.76$ V:

$$C = \frac{180-16.26}{180}\cdot\frac{1000}{2(318.76)(13.01)(50)} = 0.9097 \times 2.411\times10^{-3} = \boldsymbol{2193\ \mu\text{F}}$$

Using $V_{pk}$ for $V_o$ gives 2149 µF; either is fine if stated. Round up to the next standard value (2200 µF), since a smaller $C$ violates the ripple limit. Applied to the real ETE numbers (220 V, 500 W, 5%), the same steps give about 952 µF.

B5 — Overlap with a square-wave source [10]

A 1-φ fully controlled converter is fed from a ±250 V, 50 Hz square wave through $L_s = 3$ mH and carries a constant 15 A. At $\alpha = 30°$ (measured from the square edge), find μ and $V_o$.

model answer

During overlap all four thyristors conduct, the bridge shorts the source through $L_s$, and $v_o = 0$. The source current must swing from $-I_o$ to $+I_o$ — a change of $2I_o$ — driven by a constant $V_s$ (the square wave is flat), so the ramp is linear:

$$L_s\frac{2I_o}{t_\mu} = V_s \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu = \omega t_\mu = \frac{2\omega L_sI_o}{V_s} = \frac{2(314.16)(0.003)(15)}{250} = 0.1131\ \text{rad} = \boldsymbol{6.48°}$$

Without overlap, $v_o = -V_s$ for $\alpha$ and $+V_s$ for $\pi - \alpha$: $V_o = V_s(1 - 2\alpha/\pi) = 166.67$ V. Overlap replaces $+V_s$ by 0 for μ, losing $V_s\mu/\pi$:

$$V_o = 166.67 - \frac{250(0.1131)}{\pi} = 166.67 - 9.00 = \boldsymbol{157.7\ \text{V}}$$

No cosines anywhere. The sine-wave formula $\cos(\alpha+\mu) = \cos\alpha - 2\omega L_sI_o/V_m$ comes from integrating a sine, and does not apply to a square source. That substitution is exactly the mistake Quiz Q9 and ETE Q9 are built to catch.

B6 — Three-pulse converter charging a battery [5+5]

A 3-φ half-wave controlled converter on 415 V (line), 50 Hz charges a battery of $E = 180$ V, $r = 0.5\ \Omega$ through a large inductor. (a) Find α for 12 A. (b) At $\alpha = 30°$, what per-phase RMS supply voltage would keep 12 A?

model answer
$$V_o = E + I_or = 180 + 6 = 186\ \text{V},\qquad V_{ph} = \frac{415}{\sqrt3} = 239.6\ \text{V},\qquad V_{s,pk} = 338.85\ \text{V}$$ $$V_o = \frac{3\sqrt3\,V_{s,pk}}{2\pi}\cos\alpha = 280.2\cos\alpha \;\Longrightarrow\; \cos\alpha = 0.6638,\quad \boldsymbol{\alpha = 48.4°}$$

(b) $186 = \frac{3\sqrt3\sqrt2}{2\pi}V_{ph}\cos30° = 1.1695(0.866)V_{ph}$, so $\boldsymbol{V_{ph} = 183.6\ \text{V}}$ RMS.

The large inductor keeps the current continuous, so the $\cos\alpha$ law holds at any α (§4.2). The half-wave converter uses phase voltages, so the $\sqrt3$ in the first line is the mark the examiner is looking for.

B7 — Prove the boost DCM results [4+6]

For a boost converter with $K = 2L/RT_s$, prove (a) $K_{crit} = D(1-D)^2$ and (b) in DCM, $\dfrac{V_o}{V_{in}} = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{1+4D^2/K}}{2}$.

model answer

(a) In a boost the inductor carries the input current: $I_L = I_{in} = \frac{I_o}{1-D} = \frac{V_o}{R(1-D)}$. The ripple is $\Delta i_L = V_{in}DT_s/L$. At the boundary $I_L = \Delta i_L/2$. Substitute $V_o = V_{in}/(1-D)$:

$$\frac{V_{in}}{R(1-D)^2} = \frac{V_{in}DT_s}{2L} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{2L}{RT_s} = D(1-D)^2 = K_{crit}$$

(b) Volt-seconds: $V_{in}DT_s = (V_o - V_{in})D_2T_s$, so $D_2 = \frac{V_{in}D}{V_o - V_{in}}$. Charge: the output is fed only through the diode, which carries a falling triangle of height $I_{pk} = V_{in}DT_s/L$ for $D_2T_s$: $\frac12 I_{pk}D_2 = V_o/R$, so $D_2 = \frac{KV_o}{V_{in}D}$. Equate, with $M = V_o/V_{in}$:

$$\frac{D}{M-1} = \frac{KM}{D} \;\Longrightarrow\; M^2 - M - \frac{D^2}{K} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; M = \frac{1+\sqrt{1+4D^2/K}}{2}$$

taking the positive root, because $M > 1$ for a boost.

Sanity check at the boundary: with $K = D(1-D)^2$, $1 + 4D^2/K = 1 + \frac{4D}{(1-D)^2} = \frac{(1+D)^2}{(1-D)^2}$, so $M = \frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1+D}{1-D}\right) = \frac{1}{1-D}$ — the CCM gain. The two laws meet where they must.

B8 — Six-step inverter into a resistive star [2+3+3+2]

A 180° VSI on 600 V feeds a balanced star of 10 Ω per phase. Find the RMS phase voltage, the RMS phase current, the load power and the RMS fundamental line voltage. Sketch $v_{ab}$ and $v_{an}$ with the conducting switches marked.

model answer

The phase staircase is $\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac13$ of $V_{DC}$ in each half-cycle, each step $60°$. Mean square over a half-cycle: $\frac13\left[\frac19 + \frac49 + \frac19\right]V_{DC}^2 = \frac29V_{DC}^2$.

$$V_{ph,rms} = \frac{\sqrt2}{3}V_{DC} = \boldsymbol{282.8\ \text{V}},\qquad I_{ph,rms} = \boldsymbol{28.28\ \text{A}},\qquad P = 3\frac{282.8^2}{10} = \boldsymbol{24.0\ \text{kW}}$$ $$V_{LL,1,rms} = \frac{\sqrt6}{\pi}V_{DC} = \boldsymbol{467.8\ \text{V}}$$

Sketch: the §13.3 table. $v_{ab}$ is $+600$ for $0$–$120°$, 0 for $120$–$180°$, $-600$ for $180$–$300°$, 0 for $300$–$360°$; $v_{an}$ is the staircase 200, 400, 200, −200, −400, −200.

Note that $P$ used the total RMS, not the fundamental. A resistor heats on every harmonic. A motor's flux and torque respond to the fundamental — which is why the drive questions use 0.780, not 0.816.

15.1 The four ideas that explain the whole subject

1. A switch is lossless; a resistor is not. Fully on means no voltage; fully off means no current; either way $vi = 0$. All of power electronics is the consequence of refusing to operate anywhere in between. The only energy lost is during the transition — §2.5 — which is why switching frequency and power rating trade against each other throughout the subject.

2. In periodic steady state, the average voltage across an inductor is zero and the average current into a capacitor is zero. Not small — exactly zero, because "steady state" means the waveform repeats. This converts differential equations into arithmetic on areas, and it is the single tool that produces $V_o = DV_{in}$, $V_o = V_{in}/(1-D)$, the buck-boost ratio, the flyback ratio, the tapped-inductor ratio, and the DCM quadratic.

3. The average of a chopped waveform is set by when you chop it. A rectifier's firing angle selects which slice of the sine you keep; a converter's duty ratio selects what fraction of the period you connect. Same operation, different integrand. Getting the limits right — which device conducts over which interval — is the whole skill, which is why every chapter draws the conduction intervals before writing an integral.

4. Nothing can change instantaneously, and the consequences are everywhere. Inductor current cannot jump: that is why the diode in a buck converter must turn on when the switch opens, why commutation takes a finite overlap angle $\mu$, and why every inverter switch needs an anti-parallel diode. Capacitor voltage cannot jump: that is why a filter capacitor raises the diode PIV to $2V_{s,pk}$, and why the boost capacitor must carry the whole load for $DT_s$.

15.2 Five questions that identify any converter

Faced with a circuit you have not seen before
  1. What is the input and what is the output — AC or DC? AC→DC is a rectifier (Ch 3–5); DC→DC is a converter (Ch 6–7); DC→AC is an inverter (Ch 8–10).
  2. Are the devices controllable? All diodes → uncontrolled, no firing angle. All thyristors/switches → fully controlled. A mix → semiconverter, and it cannot invert.
  3. How many pulses per supply cycle? Count the commutations. Two for a single-phase bridge, three for a three-pulse group, six for a three-phase bridge. The pulse number sets the averaging window and the overlap drop.
  4. Where is the inductor? Series on the output side → buck. Series on the input side → boost. Shunt → buck-boost. This single question identifies the topology faster than recognising the drawing.
  5. Is the current continuous? Stiff/large $L$ or a stated constant current → continuous, use the $\cos\alpha$ or $D$ formulas. Resistive load, or a back-EMF with no inductor, or a light load → check for discontinuous conduction first.

15.4 Where marks are actually lost

  1. Measuring $\alpha$ from the wrong reference. In a three-pulse group it is counted from the natural commutation point $30°$ after the zero crossing, not from the crossing. Every limit downstream shifts.
  2. Using a $\cos\alpha$ formula on a discontinuous circuit. No inductor plus a back-EMF means the current stops. Tutorial Q4 punishes this by 17%.
  3. Memorising $\frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$ and meeting a trapezoid. Both midterms fed a non-sinusoidal input. Re-integrate.
  4. The overlap current limits. $-I_o$ to $+I_o$, a swing of $2I_o$. Integrate from $0$ to $I_o$ and everything after is halved.
  5. Averaging a switch current over its conduction time instead of the full period. The window is always $T_s$; the difference is a factor $\sqrt D$.
  6. Using the buck's $\frac18$ capacitor formula on a boost. Ask whether the output is ever disconnected: buck never, boost for $DT_s$.
  7. Forgetting which current the inductor carries. $I_o$ in a buck, $I_{in}$ in a boost, $I_{in}+I_o$ in a buck-boost. Every device formula depends on it.
  8. Slip speed scaled by speed instead of torque. At 50% torque the slip speed halves, whatever the speed.
  9. V/f scaled by rotor speeds instead of synchronous speeds. $600/1500$, not $560/1420$.
  10. Line versus phase voltage. A silent $\sqrt3$ runs straight through to the answer. The tutorial sheet itself is inconsistent — state what you assumed, every time.
  11. Sign of the commutation drop in inverting mode. It makes $|V_{dc}|$ bigger, not smaller.
  12. Not showing the method. The paper says it outright: "For numerical answers no credits will be given if you do not show your calculations."

15.6 Self-test

Conceptual

  1. A thyristor is conducting and you remove the gate signal. Nothing happens. Explain why, using the two-transistor model, and state what would turn it off.
    answer
    Each transistor supplies the other's base current, so the pair latches and the gate is no longer in the loop. Only reducing the anode current below the holding current $I_H$, and keeping it there for longer than $t_q$, will turn it off.
  2. Why can a semiconverter never invert, while a full converter can?
    answer
    Its output law is $\frac{V_{s,pk}}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha)$, and $(1+\cos\alpha)\ge0$ for every $\alpha$ — the diodes provide a freewheeling path that clamps the output at zero instead of letting it reverse. The full converter's $\cos\alpha$ goes negative past $90°$.
  3. The commutation drop $\frac{3\omega L_s I_o}{\pi}$ behaves exactly like a resistance. Why is it not one?
    answer
    Because it dissipates no power. Energy is not lost during overlap, it is merely not delivered — the output is clamped while the supply is shorted through $L_s$. A real resistance would turn that voltage into heat.
  4. In a buck converter, why must the diode conduct the instant the switch opens?
    answer
    The inductor current cannot change instantaneously. If no path existed, $di/dt$ would be enormous and the inductor would generate whatever voltage that implies — destroying the switch. The diode provides the path, which is why it is called a freewheeling diode.
  5. Raising the PWM carrier frequency does not reduce harmonic amplitudes. Why is it still worth doing?
    answer
    It moves them to frequencies where the load's own inductance presents a much higher impedance, so far less harmonic current flows. Current is what causes losses and torque ripple. The cost is proportionally higher switching loss.
  6. Why does a three-phase inverter's line voltage contain no third harmonic, when the pole voltages clearly do?
    answer
    The third-harmonic component is common to all three phases — it appears identically at $a$, $b$ and $c$ as the common-mode voltage $v_{oN} = \pm V_{DC}/6$. Taking a difference between any two phases cancels it exactly, and with no neutral it cannot drive current either.
  7. A boost converter's duty ratio is raised towards 1. The formula says $V_o\to\infty$. What actually happens, and why?
    answer
    It does not. As $D$ rises, $I_{in} = I_o/(1-D)$ rises just as fast, so the resistive losses the ideal analysis ignored grow as the square. In practice the gain peaks and then falls. Operation above about $D=0.8$ is avoided.
  8. Why is field weakening above base speed easy for an induction motor and hard for a PMSM?
    answer
    An induction motor's flux is set by $V/f$; holding $V$ while raising $f$ reduces it automatically. A PMSM's flux comes from permanent magnets and cannot be turned down, so the constant-power region needs extra current to oppose it. §11.9.
  9. A buck converter's load is made lighter until it enters DCM. Does the output ripple rise or fall?
    answer
    Typically it falls. Smaller current pulses deliver less charge per cycle to the capacitor. What gets worse is regulation: the gain now depends on the load. §13.1.

Numerical

  1. A 1-φ semiconverter on 230 V, 50 Hz feeds an RLE load, $R = 10\ \Omega$, $E = 120$ V, $\alpha = 45°$. Find $V_o$, $I_o$ and $t_c$.
    answer
    $V_{s,pk} = 325.3$, $V_o = \frac{325.3}{\pi}(1+0.7071) = 176.7$ V; $I_o = 5.67$ A; $\beta = \pi+\alpha = 225°$ so $t_c = \frac{135}{360}(20\,\text{ms}) = 7.5$ ms.
  2. A 3-φ half-wave controlled rectifier on a 400 V line (star secondary) feeds a constant 25 A at $\alpha = 45°$. Find the power delivered.
    answer
    $V_{ph} = 231.0$ rms, $V_{s,pk} = 326.6$ V. $V_o = 0.827(326.6)\cos45° = 191.0$ V. $P = 191.0 \times 25 = 4.78$ kW.
  3. A buck converter: $V_{in} = 48$ V, $V_o = 12$ V, $I_o = 4$ A, $f_s = 50$ kHz, ripple 10% of $I_o$, output ripple 1%. Find $D$, $L$, $C$ and $I_{S,rms}$.
    answer
    $D = 0.25$; $\Delta i_L = 0.4$ A; $L = \frac{12(0.75)}{0.4(50000)} = 450\ \mu$H; $\Delta V_o = 0.12$ V, $C = \frac{0.4}{8(0.12)(50000)} = 8.33\ \mu$F; $I_{S,rms} = 4\sqrt{0.25} = 2$ A.
  4. A boost converter: $V_{in} = 12$ V, $V_o = 36$ V, $P = 72$ W, $f_s = 40$ kHz, input ripple 20%, output ripple 2%. Find $D$, $L$, $C$, $I_{S,rms}$ and the diode PIV.
    answer
    $D = 1-12/36 = 0.667$; $I_{in} = 6$ A, $\Delta i_L = 1.2$ A; $I_o = 2$ A, $\Delta V_o = 0.72$ V. $L = \frac{12(0.667)}{1.2(40000)} = 167\ \mu$H; $C = \frac{2(0.667)}{0.72(40000)} = 46.3\ \mu$F; $I_{S,rms} = 6\sqrt{0.667} = 4.90$ A; PIV $= V_o = 36$ V.
  5. A 3-φ full converter, 415 V, 50 Hz, $L_s = 2$ mH, 50 A constant, $\alpha = 25°$. Find $\mu$ and the actual $V_o$.
    answer
    $V_{LL,pk} = 586.9$; $\frac{2\omega L_sI_o}{V_{LL,pk}} = \frac{62.83}{586.9} = 0.1070$; $\cos(\alpha+\mu) = 0.9063-0.1070 = 0.7993$, $\alpha+\mu = 36.94°$, $\mu = 11.9°$. $V_o = \frac{3(586.9)}{\pi}(0.9063) - \frac{3(314.16)(0.002)(50)}{\pi} = 507.9 - 30.0 = 477.9$ V.
  6. A PWM inverter has $f_c = 5$ kHz and $f_1 = 50$ Hz. List the four lowest harmonic frequencies above 4 kHz.
    answer
    $m_f = 100$. Around $m_f$ the offsets are even: $(100\pm2)50 = 4900, 5100$ and $(100\pm4)50 = 4800, 5200$. So 4800, 4900, 5100, 5200 Hz.
  7. A 6-pole, 50 Hz, 960 rpm induction motor is to run at 450 rpm at full torque under constant V/f from a 400 V rating. Find the required stator frequency and voltage.
    answer
    $N_s = 1000$ rpm, slip speed $= 40$ rpm at full torque. Required $N_s = 450+40 = 490$ rpm, so $f = 50 \times \frac{490}{1000} = 24.5$ Hz and $V_{LL} = 400 \times \frac{490}{1000} = 196$ V.
  8. A half-bridge on 100 V has its load from the leg midpoint to the negative rail. The triangular carrier runs from −2 to 8 V and the reference is 3 V. Find the average load voltage.
    answer
    $D = \frac{3-(-2)}{8-(-2)} = 0.5$, so 50 V. The carrier's offset is irrelevant once you use $D = (v_m - c_{min})/(c_{max}-c_{min})$. §10.9.
  9. A three-phase SPWM inverter runs from 650 V at $m = 0.8$. Find the RMS fundamental line and phase voltages.
    answer
    $V_{LL,1} = 0.612(0.8)(650) = 318.4$ V; $V_{ph,1} = 318.4/\sqrt3 = 183.8$ V. §10.8.

"Draw the waveform with required markings" is the most repeated instruction on every paper this examiner has set. This chapter is a bench where you can set up any of his rectifier circuits, drag α, and watch the waveform you are supposed to draw. Then draw it yourself, and check.

The lab does not use the textbook formulas — it simulates the circuit, one eighth of a degree at a time, with ideal switches. That is why it gets the awkward cases right: discontinuous current, a battery that blocks conduction until the source exceeds $E$, inverting mode, freewheeling, and the overlap notch with source inductance. A card under the plots then compares the simulation with the closed-form formula for that case and shows a ✓ when they agree. When no formula applies, it says so. That is itself a lesson.

  3. Compare segment by segment. The labels on the output trace ($v_{ab}$, $-v_s$, 0 …) and the conduction chart are the "required markings".
